\begin{abstract}
Let $F:\bits^n\to\bits^n$ have a Boolean circuit of size $s$, and put
$A=s+n^3$ and $B=A+\sqrt{n^3 2^nA}$.  Every fixed iterate $F^T$ has a
bounded-fan-in circuit of size $O(B)$, independently of the magnitude of
$T\geq0$, without assuming injectivity.  The construction uses sparse
checkpoints and sorting-based lookup, with every access and arithmetic gate
charged.  It is nonuniform and gives no efficient preprocessing algorithm
from a transition circuit.

For even $n$, an explicit linear-size permutation has almost all powers of
complexity $\Omega(2^{n/2}/n)$ under a uniform exponent modulo its order.
This matches the upper bound's exponential scale, but identifies no explicit
hard power; hard exponents necessarily have exponentially many binary digits.
The upper-bound construction also gives $O(B)$-gate cycle labels, transient
depth, pair-input reachability, exact first hitting times, and complete visit
descriptors, together with input-time and shared batched versions.

Structured transitions admit stronger bounds.  A permutation differing from
a counter at $r$ states has $O(n(r+1))$-gate signed fixed powers and navigation,
and $O(n(r+m+1))$-gate iteration for an $m$-bit input time.  Its orbit chart
can be constructed in polynomial time from an explicit edit list.  An
autonomous $q$-bit controller with a $p$-bit affine payload gives
$O(2^q(q+p^2))$-gate fixed powers.  Lean checks the circuit bounds; the
edit-list compiler remains outside that boundary.  The checkpoint and block
methods have established antecedents, and historical priority of these
circuit formulations remains unresolved.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords.} Boolean circuit complexity; function iteration;
functional graphs; nonuniform computation; sorting networks; permutation
inversion; one-way functions.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A simple transition rule can describe a computation of enormous duration.
How complicated can its final-state map be as a Boolean function of the
initial state?  We study this question for a fixed map
$F:\bits^n\to\bits^n$.  The computational resource is the total number of
Boolean gates, rather than the number of evaluations of $F$, the number of
memory probes, or the time of a random-access algorithm.

Let $N=2^n$ and suppose that $F$ has a circuit of size $s$.  Directly
unrolling $T$ applications gives a circuit of size at most $Ts$ for $F^T$.
Alternatively, hardwiring a complete truth table and testing the input
against all $N$ states gives an $O(nN)$-gate circuit, independently of $T$.
The latter bound ignores the possibility that $F$ itself is much simpler
than an arbitrary map on its state space.  Our main result exploits that
possibility.

\begin{theorem}[Fixed-power tradeoff]\label{thm:main}
Let $n\geq 1$, let $F:\bits^n\to\bits^n$ satisfy $\CC(F)\leq s$, and put
$N=2^n$ and $A=s+n^3$.  For every integer $T\geq 0$ and every integer
$1\leq L\leq N$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tradeoff}
  \CC(F^T)
  =O\!\left(Ls+n^3\left(L+\frac{N}{L}\right)\right).
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimized}
  \CC(F^T)
  =O\!\left(A+\sqrt{n^3NA}\right).
\end{equation}
The implicit constants are absolute.  The circuit may depend on $F$ and
$T$, but the size bound does not depend on the bit length of $T$.
\end{theorem}

Here and throughout, circuits use the full fan-in-two Boolean basis and
have unrestricted fan-out.  The results also hold over the basis
$\{\mathsf{AND},\mathsf{OR},\mathsf{NOT}\}$ with a constant-factor change.
Circuit size refers to the whole multi-output circuit, not to a separate
size bound for each output bit.

\begin{corollary}\label[corollary]{cor:poly}
If $\CC(F_n)=n^{O(1)}$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:half}
  \sup_{T\geq 0}\CC(F_n^T)\leq 2^{n/2+O(\log n)}.
\end{equation}
The constant hidden in $O(\log n)$ may depend on the polynomial bound for
$\CC(F_n)$, but not on $T$.
\end{corollary}

For each fixed $F$ and $T$, the circuit works for every initial state.

\subsection{Proof overview}

The functional graph of $F$ consists of directed cycles with directed
trees feeding into them.  For a parameter $L$, we select $O(N/L)$
checkpoints so that every trajectory either encounters one within $2L$
steps or repeats a state within that many steps.  A repeated state reveals
a period and permits direct recovery of the answer from the initial
trajectory.

Set $K=2L$ and assume $T\geq K$.  At each checkpoint $h$, store
$z_h=F^{T-K}(h)$.  If the initial trajectory encounters $h$ at time $d\leq K$,
then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:overview-identity}
  F^{K-d}(z_h)
  =F^{K-d}\bigl(F^{T-K}(F^d(x))\bigr)
  =F^T(x).
\end{equation}
Thus only a short \emph{forward} correction is needed after the lookup.
There is no inverse operation, so the argument applies to noninjective
maps.

A separate lookup for each of $\Theta(L)$ visited states against
$\Theta(N/L)$ checkpoints would cost $\Theta(N)$ comparisons.  Instead,
we generate the full initial trajectory before looking anything up and
resolve all its queries together.  Sorting the queries and dictionary
records reduces this cost to
$\poly(n)(L+N/L)$ Boolean gates.  Combining the initial and final short
simulations with this batched lookup gives \cref{eq:tradeoff}.

\paragraph{A worked example.}
Consider eight states, encoded by three bits, with transitions
\[
  0\to1\to2\to3\to4\to5\to2,
  \qquad 6\to7\to6.
\]
Take $L=3$, $K=6$, and $H=\{2,5\}$, the endpoints of the disjoint
three-vertex paths $(0,1,2)$ and $(3,4,5)$.  Fix $T=101$.
Starting at $x=1$, the first checkpoint is $h=2$, reached at $d=1$.
Its stored payload is $z_2=F^{95}(2)=5$, so the forward correction gives
\[
  F^{K-d}(z_2)=F^5(5)=2=F^{101}(1).
\]
Starting instead at $x=6$, the prefix $6,7,6$ repeats without meeting a
checkpoint.  The repeated indices $i=0$, $j=2$ give period $p=2$.
The answer is prefix entry $i+((101-i)\bmod p)=1$, namely $7$.
Thus the two-cycle needs no dictionary entry.

\subsection{Further results and sharpness}

The exponent $1/2$ is sharp for arbitrary fixed powers.  In
\cref{sec:lower}, an explicit linear-size permutation has almost all powers
of complexity $\Omega(2^{n/2}/n)$, when the exponent is uniform modulo its
order.  The hard powers are selected by counting, require exponentially
long binary exponents, and are not explicitly identified.  The same
permutation has a polynomial-size iterator for polynomial-width input times.

Write $B=A+\sqrt{n^3NA}$.  The extensions in \cref{sec:variable,sec:batch}
handle input exponents and shared evaluation.  The graph-query circuits
in \cref{sec:structure} compute cycle labels, transient depth, reachability,
first hitting times, visit descriptors, and synchronous coalescence in
$O(B)$ gates.  Pair queries take two $n$-bit states; their exponential
scale remains $2^{n/2}$ for polynomial-size transitions.
Recognizable targets include eventual membership and recurring or permanent
acceptance.  For a predicate of size $a$, put $A_P=s+a+n^3$ and
$B_P=A_P+\sqrt{n^3NA_P}$.

\Cref{sec:structured} gives stronger bounds for autonomous controllers and
sparse counter edits.  For the latter, an explicit edit list permits
polynomial-time chart construction; transport to other coordinates charges
their encoding and decoding circuits.  Choosing $T=\ord(P)-1$ also recovers
the earlier square-root permutation-inversion bound, compatible with
polynomial-size one-wayness; see \cref{sec:one-way}.

\subsection{Related work and status of the formulation}\label{sec:related}

Preprocessing trajectories to exchange storage for later computation is
classical.  Hellman's cryptanalytic time--memory tradeoff is an early
example of this approach~\cite{Hellman80}.  More directly, Munro, Raman,
Raman, and Rao~\cite[Section~3.1]{MRRR12} give a shortcut representation
for permutation inversion: with a parameter $L$, their black-box
construction uses $O(L)$ evaluations and
$O((N/L)(\log N+\log L))$ auxiliary bits.  They also give succinct
representations of general functions supporting positive powers with a
word-size exponent~\cite[Section~5]{MRRR12}.  Those results concern
representation space, RAM time, and black-box access, rather than total
Boolean gate count.

A closer circuit antecedent is Hirahara, Ilango, and
Williams~\cite[Section~3.2]{HIW24}: they obtain square-root-size
permutation-inversion circuits using checkpoints and batched lookup.
Their lookup theorem builds on a lemma of Paul.  This draft generalizes
that permutation-inversion construction to arbitrary fixed forward powers
of possibly noninjective maps.  It does not subsume their general-function
inversion and compression theorems.

Eppstein~\cite{Eppstein23} studies uniform iteration of polynomial-time
bijections, with the iteration count supplied in binary, and characterizes
the resulting class of functional problems as
$\mathsf{FP}^{\mathsf{PSPACE}}$.  Our resource question differs: the map
is fixed, the circuits are nonuniform, and the size bound remains
exponential in the state length.  Our result supplies no polynomial-time
algorithm for that iteration problem.

The present construction uses the same checkpoint and batched-lookup
principles.  Batcher's sorting networks~\cite{Batcher68} implement the
lookup; the asymptotically smaller networks of Ajtai, Koml\'os, and
Szemer\'edi~\cite{AKS83} save a factor of $n$ (\cref{sec:aks}), while
the shifted payload in \cref{eq:overview-identity} and the
checkpoint-or-repetition lemma handle general powers and functional graphs.
No RAM memory probe is treated as a unit-cost circuit operation.
Checkpointing, sorting-based lookup, and square-root-size permutation
inversion are not claimed as new.  Historical priority for the full
fixed-power theorem and the shared bounds in this draft remains unresolved.

\section{Model and circuit primitives}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Conventions}

All logarithms are to base two.  The notation $F^t$ denotes $t$-fold
composition, with $F^0$ equal to the identity.  The quantity $\CC(G)$ is the
minimum number of internal gates in an acyclic Boolean circuit computing
all output bits of $G$.  Each internal gate computes a Boolean function
of at most two bits.  Inputs, wires, fan-out, and output designations are
not charged as gates.  The two constant bits are available; charging a
constant number of gates for them would not change any bound.

The circuit for an iterate is allowed to depend on the entire function
$F$ and on its fixed exponent.  Preprocessing time and the time required
to find the circuit are not bounded.  Hardwired data do not provide free
addressing: every comparison, multiplexer, and routing operation used to
access them is realized with Boolean gates and included in our estimates.

All state words have $n$ bits.  Because $L\leq 2^n$, indices up to $2L$ fit
in $n+2$ bits.  In the batched construction $r\leq 2^n$, so query identifiers
and record positions also have $O(n)$ bits.  We retain explicit polynomial
factors in $n$ for a concrete implementation; these factors are not
claimed to be optimal.

\subsection{Arithmetic and selection}

Comparison, addition, subtraction, and conditional selection of $w$-bit
words have $O(w)$-gate circuits.  Binary long division gives an
$O(w^2)$-gate circuit for the remainder of a $w$-bit integer modulo a
positive $w$-bit integer: process the dividend bits from most to least
significant, and at each stage conditionally subtract the modulus from
the current shifted remainder.  There are $O(w)$ stages and $O(w)$ gates
per stage.

A circuit can select one of $q$ words of width $w$ using $O(qw)$ gates by
a binary tree of word multiplexers, padding to a power of two when
necessary.  It can also select one flagged record from an ordered list of
$q$ records in $O(qw)$ gates.  For example, a left-to-right scan maintains
a bit indicating whether a flagged record has already been found and
retains the first one.  This scan is simply an acyclic chain of gates;
no depth bound is needed here.

\begin{lemma}[Batched fixed-dictionary lookup]\label[lemma]{lem:lookup}
Suppose a fixed dictionary consists of $M$ distinct $w$-bit keys, each
with a $w$-bit payload.  Given $Q\geq 1$ query keys, a Boolean circuit can
output a match bit and the corresponding payload for every query, in the
original query order, using
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lookup-general}
  O\!\left((M+Q)\bigl(w+\log(M+Q+2)\bigr)
                    \log^2(M+Q+2)\right)
\end{equation}
gates.  The payload of an unmatched query can be set to zero.  In
particular, if $w=O(n)$ and $\log(M+Q+2)=O(n)$, the cost is
$O(n^3(M+Q))$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $R=M+Q$.  Form one record for each dictionary entry and one for each
query.  A record contains a key, a dictionary/query tag, a payload field,
and an original-position field.  The record width is
$O(w+\log(R+2))$.

Sort the records lexicographically by key, then by the tag with dictionary
records first, and then by original position.  A sorting network with
$O(R\log^2(R+2))$ comparators exists~\cite{Batcher68}.  A comparator
and the resulting conditional swap of the entire records cost
$O(w+\log(R+2))$ Boolean gates.  Padding uses a separate dummy-record bit
so that no actual key is reserved as a sentinel.

In the sorted list, a dictionary record precedes every query with its key.
Scan the list, resetting the match bit when the key changes and propagating
the payload from a dictionary record through the equal-key run that
follows it.  If an equal-key run has no dictionary record, every query in
that run receives match bit zero.  Dictionary keys are distinct, so no
run contains two competing payloads.  This scan uses
$O(R(w+\log(R+2)))$ gates.

Finally, sort records by a tag that places queries first and then by the
query's original position.  The first $Q$ records give the requested
outputs in order.  A second sorting network has the same asymptotic cost
as the first.  Every operation described is a fixed acyclic circuit.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Detecting a repeated state]\label[lemma]{lem:repeat}
Given $Q$ words of $w$ bits, a circuit can return a flag indicating whether
two words are equal and, when the flag is one, indices $i<j$ of two equal
words.  Its size is
\[
  O\!\left(Q\bigl(w+\log(Q+2)\bigr)\log^2(Q+2)\right).
\]
In particular, for $Q\leq 2^{n+O(1)}$ and $w=O(n)$, the size is $O(n^3Q)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Attach each word's original index and sort by word and then by index.
A duplicate exists exactly when two adjacent sorted records have the
same word.  Compare adjacent words and select one flagged pair.  Sorting
by index within an equal-word run guarantees that the returned indices
satisfy $i<j$.  The remaining comparisons and selection use at most
$O(Q(w+\log(Q+2)))$ gates.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Sparse checkpoints in functional graphs}\label{sec:checkpoints}

The functional graph of a map $F$ has one vertex for each state and the
edge $v\to F(v)$ at every vertex.  Each weakly connected component has a
unique directed cycle, with directed trees feeding into it.  For a
noncycle vertex, its \emph{depth} is the number of steps needed to reach
the cycle.

\begin{lemma}[Checkpoint or repetition]\label[lemma]{lem:checkpoints}
Let $F$ be a function on a set of $N$ states, and let $1\leq L\leq N$.
There is a set $H$ of at most $\floor{N/L}$ states such that, for every $x$,
either two of $x,F(x),\ldots,F^{L-1}(x)$ are equal or
$F^d(x)\in H$ for some $d<2L$.
More generally, the same conclusion holds for starting states in any
finite forward-invariant set $S$, with $|H|L\leq |S|$ and $H\subseteq S$.
The ambient state type need not be finite.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Choose a maximal family of pairwise vertex-disjoint simple directed paths,
each consisting of $L$ vertices in $S$.  Put the last vertex of each path
into $H$.  Disjointness gives $|H|L\leq |S|$.
If the first $L$ states of an orbit are distinct, they form such a path
in $S$.  By maximality this path meets a chosen path: say
$F^i(x)=F^j(a)$, where $i,j<L$ and the chosen path starts at $a$.
Its marked endpoint is reached from $x$ after
$i+(L-1-j)<2L$ steps.  If the prefix is not simple, it already contains a
repetition.  Taking $S$ to be the entire state space proves the first
claim.  The Lean proof implements the same packing argument by induction
on $|S|$, removing one simple path at a time.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
It is essential that the lemma allows a short repetition instead of a
checkpoint.  A function can have $N$ fixed points, in which case a set
meeting every eventual cycle must contain all $N$ states.  Such cycles
need no stored shortcut: their behavior is already visible locally.
\end{remark}

\section{Circuits for fixed powers}\label{sec:fixed}

We now prove \cref{thm:main}.  The construction is uniform as a description
of a circuit from the chosen constants, but no efficient procedure for
computing those constants is asserted.

\subsection{Short trajectories and shifted payloads}

Fix $L$ and let $K=2L$.  If $T<K$, use $T$ copies of a circuit for $F$;
this costs at most $2Ls$ gates.  Hence assume $T\geq K$.
Choose $H$ as in \cref{lem:checkpoints}.  For every $h\in H$, hardwire the
$n$-bit payload
\begin{equation}\label{eq:payload}
  z_h=F^{T-K}(h).
\end{equation}
The dictionary is fixed independently of the input $x$ and has at most
$N/L$ entries.

On input $x$, first compute
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prefix}
  x_0=x,\quad x_1=F(x),\quad\ldots,\quad x_K=F^K(x).
\end{equation}
This requires $Ks$ gates.  Apply \cref{lem:lookup} to all $K+1$ states in
\cref{eq:prefix}, using the dictionary $h\mapsto z_h$.  Select one
matching record, if any, and retain its original trajectory index $d$.
This gives a match flag and a pair $(d,z_h)$ with $x_d=h$ whenever the
flag is one.

For the matched case, compute a second short trajectory
\[
  z_h,F(z_h),\ldots,F^K(z_h)
\]
and select its entry at position $K-d$.  Its value is
\begin{align}
  F^{K-d}(z_h)
    &=F^{K-d}\bigl(F^{T-K}(h)\bigr)\notag\\
    &=F^{T-d}(h)\notag\\
    &=F^{T-d}\bigl(F^d(x)\bigr)
     =F^T(x).\label{eq:identity}
\end{align}
All exponents used here are nonnegative.  This second trajectory costs
another $Ks$ gates; selection costs $O(nK)$ gates.

\subsection{The repeated-state case and very large exponents}

Use \cref{lem:repeat} on \cref{eq:prefix}.  If no checkpoint was found,
\cref{lem:checkpoints} guarantees indices
\[
  0\leq i<j\leq K,\qquad x_i=x_j.
\]
Put $p=j-i$.  Determinism implies $x_{u+p}=x_u$ for all $u\geq i$.
The number $p$ need not be the least period.  Since $T\geq K\geq i$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:period}
  F^T(x)=x_{\,i+((T-i)\bmod p)}.
\end{equation}
The selected index is at most $i+p-1=j-1$, so the required state already
occurs in the initial trajectory.

To implement \cref{eq:period} without paying for the bit length of $T$,
hardwire the table
\begin{equation}\label{eq:residue-table}
  R_T(p)=T\bmod p,\qquad 1\leq p\leq K.
\end{equation}
There are $K$ entries, each of $O(n)$ bits.  A multiplexer tree retrieves
$R_T(p)$ in $O(nK)$ gates.  Compute $i\bmod p$ by binary long division
and then compute
\[
  (T-i)\bmod p
    =\bigl(R_T(p)-(i\bmod p)\bigr)\bmod p
\]
using a comparison, subtraction, and a conditional addition of $p$.
These operations take $O(n^2)$ gates.  A further $O(nK)$ gates select the
state prescribed in \cref{eq:period}.

A circuit evaluates both branches, so all intermediate operations must
also be defined when their output will be discarded.  If a repeated pair
is absent, supply the dummy values $i=0$ and $p=1$ to the arithmetic
branch.  If a checkpoint is absent, supply a dummy state and $d=0$ to
the second trajectory.  The final output selects the checkpoint branch
when its match flag is one, and otherwise selects the repetition branch.
The checkpoint lemma ensures that the selected branch is always valid.
In particular, no division by zero occurs on any input.

\subsection{Size bound}

The two simulations together use at most $2Ks=4Ls$ gates.  The dictionary
and query list contain
\[
  |H|+(K+1)=O\!\left(\frac{N}{L}+L\right)
\]
records of $O(n)$ bits, and the logarithm of this number is $O(n)$.
Thus \cref{lem:lookup} costs
$O(n^3(N/L+L))$ gates.  Repeated-state detection costs $O(n^3L)$ gates,
and all remaining lookup, arithmetic, and selection operations cost
$O(nL+n^2)$ gates.  Since $n,L\geq 1$, the latter cost is absorbed by
$O(n^3L)$.  This proves \cref{eq:tradeoff}.

Write the tradeoff as $O(AL+n^3N/L)$, where $A=s+n^3$, and choose
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L-choice}
  L=\max\left\{1,\ceil{\sqrt{n^3N/A}}\right\}.
\end{equation}
Since $A\geq n^3$, this choice satisfies $L\leq\ceil{\sqrt N}\leq N$.
If $A\leq n^3N$, then
$\sqrt{n^3N/A}\leq L\leq 2\sqrt{n^3N/A}$.  Hence
\[
  AL=O(\sqrt{n^3NA}),\qquad
  n^3N/L=O(\sqrt{n^3NA}).
\]
If $A>n^3N$, the choice is $L=1$ and the cost is $O(A)$.
This establishes
\cref{eq:optimized} and completes the proof of \cref{thm:main}.
\hfill$\square$

\begin{remark}[Where the exponent is stored]\label[remark]{rem:nonuniform}
For $T\geq K$, the information about $T$ used by the circuit is confined
to the checkpoint payloads in \cref{eq:payload} and the residue table in
\cref{eq:residue-table}.  Their total length is
$O(n(N/L+L))$ bits.  The construction does not place the binary expansion
of $T$ on a long hidden input tape, and it does not perform uncharged
arithmetic on an arbitrarily large integer.  Computing the hardwired
information is a preprocessing task outside the asserted circuit bound.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Charging only long trajectories}

Let $\delta(v)$ be the distance from $v$ to its eventual cycle, and let
$\ell(v)$ be that cycle's length.  For $1\leq L\leq N$, put
\[
 Q_L=|\{v:\delta(v)\geq L\}|+
     |\{v:\delta(v)=0,\ \ell(v)\geq L\}|.
\]
The two sets are disjoint.  Their union contains the deep transient
vertices and all vertices of cycles with length at least $L$.

\begin{theorem}[Long-trajectory tradeoff]\label{thm:active-mass}
For every fixed $T\geq0$ and $1\leq L\leq N$,
\[
 \CC(F^T)=O\!\left(Ls+n^3\left(L+\frac{Q_L}{L}\right)\right).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply the path-packing argument of \cref{lem:checkpoints} only to paths
whose $L$ vertices all lie in the union above.  Mark their endpoints.
The resulting set $H$ satisfies $|H|L\leq Q_L$.  Every $L$-state prefix
inside this union either repeats or meets a packed path, and hence reaches
its marked endpoint within $2L$ steps.  This packing argument does not
require the union to be forward invariant.

If $\delta(x)\geq2L$, the first $L$ states are all deep, so that argument
applies immediately.  Otherwise the trajectory reaches its cycle before
$2L$ steps.  A short cycle then repeats before $3L$ steps.  On a long
cycle all vertices lie in the union, so the packing argument gives a
checkpoint or repetition within another $2L$ steps.  Thus every trajectory
hits $H$ or repeats within a prefix of $4L$ steps.

Use this prefix in the fixed-power construction, with payload
$F^{T-4L}(h)$ when $T\geq4L$ and direct unrolling otherwise.  The
dictionary has at most $Q_L/L$ entries and the prefix has $O(L)$ states,
giving the stated cost.
\end{proof}

Short transient paths leading to short cycles incur no dictionary cost.
The bound includes $Q_L=0$.  Since $Q_L$ itself depends on $L$, one must
retain that dependence when choosing the tradeoff parameter.

\subsection{Invariant sets and image-sensitive bounds}

\begin{theorem}[Invariant-set tradeoff]\label{thm:invariant}
Let $S\subseteq\bits^n$ be forward invariant under $F$, with $|S|=M$.
For every fixed $T\geq0$ and $1\leq L\leq N$, there is a circuit agreeing
with $F^T$ on $S$ of size
\[
 O\!\left(Ls+n^3\left(L+\frac{M}{L}\right)\right).
\]
The circuit is total on $\bits^n$; correctness outside $S$ is not asserted.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Use the invariant-set version of \cref{lem:checkpoints}, and otherwise
apply the fixed-power construction unchanged.  A state still occupies
$n$ bits, but the dictionary contains only $O(M/L)$ records.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Image sensitivity]\label[corollary]{cor:image}
Put $M_d=|\operatorname{im}(F^d)|$.  For $T\geq d$ and $1\leq L\leq M_d$,
\[
 \CC(F^T)\leq \CC(F^d)+
 O\!\left(Ls+n^3\left(L+\frac{M_d}{L}\right)\right).
\]
Consequently this is at most
$\CC(F^d)+O(A+\sqrt{n^3M_dA})$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The image of $F^d$ is forward invariant, since
$F(F^d(x))=F^d(F(x))$.  Compute $F^d(x)$ and apply
\cref{thm:invariant} on its image for $T-d$ more steps.
Optimize $L$ as in \cref{eq:L-choice}, replacing $N$ by $M_d$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Permutation inversion and one-way functions}\label{sec:one-way}

\begin{corollary}[Exact permutation inversion]\label[corollary]{cor:inverse}
Let $P:\bits^n\to\bits^n$ be a permutation with $\CC(P)\leq s$, and put
$N=2^n$ and $A=s+n^3$.  For every $1\leq L\leq N$,
\[
  \CC(P^{-1})
  =O\!\left(Ls+n^3\left(L+\frac NL\right)\right).
\]
In particular,
\[
  \CC(P^{-1})=O\!\left(A+\sqrt{n^3NA}\right).
\]
If $s=n^{O(1)}$, an inverter of size $2^{n/2+O(\log n)}$ is correct on
every input.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let $D=\ord(P)$, the least common multiple of its cycle lengths.
Then $P^D$ is the identity, so $P^{-1}=P^{D-1}$.
Apply \cref{thm:main}.  The exponent may have a long binary expansion,
but the theorem charges only the resulting tables and their access gates.
\end{proof}

This recovers the permutation-inversion circuit bound already discussed
by Hirahara, Ilango, and Williams~\cite[Section~3.2]{HIW24}.
It identifies an earlier special case of the fixed-power theorem.

One-wayness against polynomial-size circuits requires every such inverter
to have negligible success on the image of a uniform input
\cite[Section~7.1.1]{Goldreich08}.  The exponential-size exact inverter above
is compatible with that requirement.  It does rule out security against
all circuits of size $2^{(1/2+\varepsilon)n}$, for any fixed
$\varepsilon>0$ and sufficiently large $n$; it establishes no security
at smaller sizes.

For noninjective maps, a forward power need not find a preimage.  For
example, the map with values $(0,0,1,0)$ on $\{0,1,2,3\}$ has unique
preimage $2$ of $1$, while the forward orbit of $1$ stays in $\{0,1\}$.
Hirahara, Ilango, and Williams give a separate general-function inversion
bound of $2^{4n/5}\poly(s+n)$ gates~\cite[Theorem~3.5]{HIW24}.
Encoding exhaustive preimage search as iteration retains both an $n$-bit
target and an $n$-bit candidate, so the present theorem gives only
$2^{n+O(\log n)}$ gates for that construction.

Nor does the counting example in \cref{sec:lower} establish one-way
functions.  Its update permutation has a linear-size inverse, obtained
by decrementing the active cycle coordinate.  The selected hard powers
have exponential worst-case forward complexity, so cannot themselves
form a polynomial-time one-way function.  The counting argument gives
no average-case inversion hardness.

\section{A matching exponent for arbitrary fixed powers}\label{sec:lower}

This section gives an explicit linear-size permutation whose collection
of powers is large enough that some member has circuit complexity at
least $2^{n/2}/\poly(n)$.  The counting argument is elementary and does
not require an asymptotic estimate for the maximal order of a permutation.

\subsection{A permutation with cycles of all lengths up to \texorpdfstring{$2^{n/2}-1$}{2 to the power n/2, minus 1}}

Let $n=2k$ and $B=2^k-1$.  Write a state as a pair of $k$-bit integers
$(a,b)$.  Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:permutation}
 P_n(a,b)=
 \begin{cases}
   (a,b+1\bmod a),&1\leq a\leq B\ \text{and}\ 0\leq b<a,\\
   (a,b),&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
For each $1\leq a\leq B$, the states
$(a,0),(a,1),\ldots,(a,a-1)$ form one cycle of length $a$; every other
state is fixed.  Hence $P_n$ is a permutation and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:order}
  \ord(P_n)=D_B:=\lcm(1,2,\ldots,B).
\end{equation}

Despite the notation in \cref{eq:permutation}, the update does not require
a general division circuit.  Compare $b$ with $a$, increment $b$, and,
when $b<a$, replace $b$ by zero exactly when the increment equals $a$.
Otherwise retain the appropriate increment or original value.
Comparison, increment, and selection each have $O(k)$-gate circuits.
The first coordinate is passed through unchanged.  Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linear-update}
  \CC(P_n)=O(n),
\end{equation}
and the family has a direct polynomial-time circuit construction.

\begin{theorem}[Powers and fixed-integer remainders]\label{thm:remainders}
For a fixed $T\geq0$, define $r_T(a)=T\bmod a$ when $a>0$ and
$r_T(0)=0$, using $k$ bits for both input and output.  Then
\[
  \CC(r_T)\leq\CC(P_n^T)\leq\CC(r_T)+O(n).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Restrict the power circuit to inputs $(a,0)$ and retain its second
coordinate.  This computes $r_T$, including the zero-modulus branch.
Conversely, on an active state compute $b+r_T(a)$ using $k+1$ bits.
Both summands are below $a$, so a comparison and at most one subtraction
of $a$ give the result modulo $a$.  The spare bit retains the carry.
Select the original $b$ on inactive states and copy $a$ to the output.
All additional operations have linear-size Boolean circuits.  The first
inequality uses the paper's free constant-wire convention; the native
Lean model charges one shared zero gate for the restriction.
\end{proof}

Thus the arithmetic object behind the lower bound is the remainder of a
single fixed integer as a function of its input modulus.  The theorem
does not identify a particular hard integer.

\begin{lemma}\label[lemma]{lem:lcm}
For every integer $B\geq 1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lcm-lower}
  \lcm(1,2,\ldots,B)\geq \frac{2^B}{B+1}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We show that every binomial coefficient $\binom Bj$ divides $D_B$.
For a prime $p$, factorial valuations give
\[
 \nu_p\!\binom Bj
 =\sum_{a\geq 1}
   \left(\floor{\frac{B}{p^a}}-
         \floor{\frac{j}{p^a}}-
         \floor{\frac{B-j}{p^a}}\right).
\]
Each summand is zero or one, and it is zero when $p^a>B$.
Therefore
\[
  \nu_p\!\binom Bj\leq \floor{\log_p B}=\nu_p(D_B),
\]
where both sides are zero for $p>B$.  This proves divisibility.
Since $\sum_{j=0}^B\binom Bj=2^B$, some binomial coefficient is at least
$2^B/(B+1)$, and $D_B$ is at least that large.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Counting multi-output circuits]\label[lemma]{lem:count}
The number of functions $\bits^n\to\bits^n$ computable with at most $q$
fan-in-two Boolean gates is at most
\begin{equation}\label{eq:count}
  (q+1)16^q(n+q+2)^{2q+n}
  =2^{O((q+n)\log(n+q+2))}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Topologically order the gates.  At each gate choose one of at most $16$
Boolean operations and two predecessors among the inputs, the two
constants, and gates.  Allowing all $n+q+2$ possible predecessors at each
position only increases the count.  Choose the $n$ output wires from
these same possibilities.  Sum over gate counts from zero through $q$;
each summand is bounded by the expression without its factor $q+1$.
Different encodings may compute the same function, which only helps the
upper bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Hard powers of a simple permutation]\label{thm:lower}
For all sufficiently large even $n$, the permutation in
\cref{eq:permutation} satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hard-power}
  \max_{T\geq 0}\CC(P_n^T)
    =\Omega\!\left(\frac{2^{n/2}}{n}\right).
\end{equation}
Together with \cref{thm:main}, this gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tight-exponent}
  \log_2\!\left(\max_{T\geq 0}\CC(P_n^T)\right)
    =\frac n2+O(\log n).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
There are exactly $D_B$ distinct powers of $P_n$, with representatives
$0\leq T<D_B$.  By \cref{lem:lcm},
\[
  \log_2 D_B\geq B-\log_2(B+1).
\]
Let $q=\floor{cB/n}$ for a sufficiently small positive absolute constant
$c$.  Since $B=2^{n/2}-1$, \cref{lem:count} bounds the logarithm of the
number of circuits of size at most $q$ by
\[
  O((q+n)\log(n+q+2))=O(cB+n^2).
\]
Choose $c$ so that the coefficient of $B$ is less than $1/2$.
For all sufficiently large even $n$, this quantity is smaller than
$B-\log_2(B+1)$.  Thus there are fewer size-$q$ circuits than distinct
powers of $P_n$.  At least one power has complexity greater than $q$,
proving \cref{eq:hard-power}.

For the upper bound, substitute $\CC(P_n)=O(n)$ into
\cref{eq:optimized}.  This gives
$\max_T\CC(P_n^T)=O(n^3 2^{n/2})$.  Combining the upper and lower
bounds and taking logarithms proves \cref{eq:tight-exponent}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Almost every power is hard]\label{thm:typical-powers}
There is an absolute $c>0$ such that, for all sufficiently large even $n$,
\[
 \Pr_{T\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{0,\ldots,D_B-1\}}
 \left[\CC(P_n^T)\geq c\frac{2^{n/2}}n\right]
 \geq 1-2^{-\Omega(2^{n/2})}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Set $U=2^{n/2}$.  The preceding counting argument bounds the number of
powers with circuits of size at most $\floor{cU/n}$ by $2^{\alpha U}$,
for some fixed $\alpha<1$ after decreasing $c$.  On the other hand,
$D_B\geq2^{U-1}/U$.  Divide the two counts to bound the exceptional
fraction by $U2^{1-(1-\alpha)U}=2^{-\Omega(U)}$.
\end{proof}

The probability concerns exponent residues modulo the whole group order.
It does not concern short exponent descriptions or average-case hardness
over inputs to any one power.  In particular, easy powers remain among
the exceptional residues.

\begin{corollary}\label[corollary]{cor:one-output}
For all sufficiently large even $n$, some power of $P_n$ has an output
coordinate of circuit complexity $\Omega(2^{n/2}/n^2)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For the power supplied by \cref{thm:lower}, join optimal circuits for all
$n$ output coordinates.  Their total size is at most $n$ times the
largest coordinate complexity and is an upper bound on the complexity
of the whole map.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Why the lower bound does not apply to short input times}
\label{sec:lower-limits}

The lower bound selects $T$ from $\{0,\ldots,D_B-1\}$; it places no
polynomial bound on the bit length of that exponent.  Indeed,
\cref{lem:lcm} shows that $\log D_B\geq 2^{n/2}-O(n)$.
The choice of a power therefore has exponentially many available
bits.  The theorem does not provide a polynomial-time procedure that,
given $n$, identifies a hard power.

For the very same family, the iterator with an $m$-bit time input has
size $O(n(m+1))$.  On an active cycle,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:easy-iterator}
  P_n^t(a,b)=(a,(b+(t\bmod a))\bmod a).
\end{equation}
An $m$-stage remainder computation with a $k$-bit running remainder costs
$O(mk)$ gates, and the remaining comparison, addition, and selection
cost $O(n)$ gates as in \cref{thm:remainders}.  On inactive states the answer is the
input itself; use a dummy positive modulus when $a=0$.
Consequently, for $m=\poly(n)$ this iterator has polynomial size.

\begin{corollary}[Bit length of hard exponents]\label[corollary]{cor:exponent-length}
Let $T_n$ be exponents satisfying
$\CC(P_n^{T_n})=\Omega(2^{n/2}/n)$ for sufficiently large even $n$.
Their binary lengths
$m_n=\max\{1,\ceil{\log_2(T_n+1)}\}$ satisfy
\[
  m_n=\Omega(2^{n/2}/n^2).
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Hardwire the $m_n$-bit exponent into the iterator just described.
This gives $\CC(P_n^{T_n})=O(n(m_n+1))$.
Comparing with the assumed lower bound proves the claim.
\end{proof}

Thus the hard powers necessarily require exponentially long binary
exponents; long descriptions are not merely allowed by the counting proof.
Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} establishes sharpness for arbitrary fixed powers.
It gives no hardness result for the bounded-bit-time problem.

\subsection{Explicit hard exponents}\label{sec:explicit-exponents}

By \cref{cor:exponent-length}, a hard power of $P_n$ has an exponent
with $2^{n/2}/\poly(n)$ binary digits.  The natural notion of an explicit
hard power therefore allows time polynomial in that length.  Call a
sequence of exponents $(T_n)_{n\ \mathrm{even}}$ \emph{explicit} if a
deterministic algorithm outputs the binary expansion of $T_n$ from $1^n$
in time $2^{O(n)}$.  Write
$\mathsf{E}=\mathsf{DTIME}(2^{O(\ell)})$ for languages of strings of
length $\ell$, and $\mathsf{SIZE}(s(\ell))$ for languages whose length-$\ell$
slices have circuits of size $s(\ell)$.

\begin{theorem}[Explicit hard powers give circuit lower bounds for
\texorpdfstring{$\mathsf{E}$}{E}]\label{thm:explicit-E}
Let $\varepsilon>0$, and let $(T_n)$ be explicit exponents with
$\CC(P_n^{T_n})\geq 2^{\varepsilon n}$ for infinitely many even $n$.
Then for every $0<\delta<\varepsilon$ there is a language in $\mathsf{E}$
whose length-$\ell$ slice requires circuits of size $2^{\delta\ell}$ for
infinitely many $\ell$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For even $n=2k$ put $j_n=\ceil{\log_2 n}$ and $\ell_n=n+j_n$; these
lengths are strictly increasing in $n$.  A string of length $\ell_n$ is
read as $(a,b,i)$ with $a,b\in\bits^k$ and $i\in\bits^{j_n}$.  Let $L$
contain $(a,b,i)$ exactly when $i<n$ and bit $i$ of $P_n^{T_n}(a,b)$ is
one, and let $L$ contain no strings of other lengths.

To decide $L$, recover $n$ from $\ell$, compute $T_n$ in time $2^{O(n)}$,
and evaluate $P_n^{T_n}(a,b)$ by \cref{eq:easy-iterator}, returning
$(a,b)$ on inactive states.  The remainder $T_n\bmod a$ of an integer with
$2^{O(n)}$ digits by a $k$-bit modulus takes time $2^{O(n)}$ by long
division.  Hence $L\in\mathsf{E}$.

Conversely, hardwiring $i=0,\ldots,n-1$ into $n$ copies of a circuit for
the slice $L_{\ell_n}$ computes all output bits of $P_n^{T_n}$.  Thus
$\CC(L_{\ell_n})\geq \CC(P_n^{T_n})/n\geq 2^{\varepsilon n}/n$ for the
infinitely many $n$ in the hypothesis.  Since $\ell_n\leq n+\log_2 n+1$,
this exceeds $2^{\delta\ell_n}$ for all sufficiently large such $n$.
\end{proof}

Unconditionally exhibiting an explicit hard power of $P_n$ is therefore
at least as hard as proving an exponential circuit lower bound for
$\mathsf{E}$, an open problem; the hypothesis that such a lower bound holds
is the hardness assumption of Impagliazzo and Wigderson~\cite{IW97}.  This
complements \cref{thm:typical-powers}: almost every residue modulo
$D_B$ is hard, but no efficient procedure is known for producing one.

The amount of hardwired information is also forced.  Distinct residues of
$T$ modulo $D_B$ give distinct powers, and \cref{thm:remainders} recovers
the remainder table $r_T$, hence $T\bmod D_B$, from $P_n^T$.  Any
description of $P_n^T$ from which the map can be evaluated, whether a
circuit, a table, or a checkpoint dictionary, therefore determines
$T\bmod D_B$.  By \cref{lem:lcm}, for some $T$ such a description has at
least $\log_2 D_B\geq 2^{n/2}-O(n)$ bits.  The dictionary of
\cref{sec:fixed}, with $O(n2^{n/2})$ bits at the balanced parameter,
is within a polynomial factor of this minimum.

\paragraph{The converse direction.}
Does an exponential lower bound for $\mathsf{E}$ conversely yield an
explicit hard power of $P_n$?  The obvious encoding gives a conditional
form, which an index-to-prime circuit makes unconditional for lower bounds
above $2^{\ell/2}$.  Let $q_m(z)$ denote the $(z+1)$st prime, for
$z\in\bits^m$ read as an integer.  By the prime number theorem
$q_m(z)<2^{m+\log_2 m+O(1)}$, and the map $q_m$ is computable in time
$2^{O(m)}$ by a sieve.  Let $k_m$ be the least integer with
$2^{k_m}-1\geq q_m(2^m-1)$; then $k_m=m+\log_2 m+O(1)$, and we regard
$q_m$ as a map from $\bits^m$ to $\bits^{k_m}$.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional converse]\label[proposition]{prop:explicit-converse}
Let $L\in\mathsf{E}$ satisfy $\CC(L_m)\geq 2^{\varepsilon m}$ for
infinitely many $m$, and suppose that $\CC(q_m)\leq 2^{\varepsilon m-1}$
for all sufficiently large $m$.  Then there are explicit exponents $T_n$
such that $\CC(P_n^{T_n})\geq 2^{\varepsilon m-1}$ for $n=2k_m$ and
infinitely many $m$.  In particular, for every fixed
$\varepsilon'<\varepsilon/2$, $\CC(P_n^{T_n})\geq 2^{\varepsilon' n}$ for
infinitely many even $n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The map $m\mapsto k_m$ is strictly increasing.  For $n=2k_m$, let $T_n$
be the least nonnegative integer with $T_n\equiv L(z)\pmod{q_m(z)}$ for
every $z\in\bits^m$, and put $T_n=0$ for all other even $n$.  The moduli
are distinct primes, so the Chinese remainder theorem gives $T_n$.  It is
computable in time $2^{O(m)}=2^{O(n)}$ from the truth table of $L_m$ and
the $2^m$ primes, so $(T_n)$ is explicit.

Given a circuit for $r_{T_n}$ on $k_m$-bit moduli, compose it with a
circuit for $q_m$.  On input $z$ the result outputs
$T_n\bmod q_m(z)=L(z)\in\{0,1\}$ in its low bit.  Therefore
\[
  \CC(P_n^{T_n})\geq\CC(r_{T_n})\geq \CC(L_m)-\CC(q_m)
  \geq 2^{\varepsilon m-1}
\]
for the infinitely many large $m$ in the hypothesis, by
\cref{thm:remainders}.  Since $n=2m+O(\log m)$, this is at least
$2^{\varepsilon' n}$ for any fixed $\varepsilon'<\varepsilon/2$ and all
large such $m$.
\end{proof}

Hardwiring the table of $q_m$ costs about $2^m$ gates, which exceeds the
complexity of every $m$-input function, so the hypothesis on $q_m$ needs a
genuine construction.  Counting primes in blocks gives one at the square
root of the table size.  Write $\pi(x)$ for the number of primes at most
$x$.

\begin{lemma}[Index-to-prime circuits]\label[lemma]{lem:prime-index}
For every $k\geq 2$ there is a circuit with $k$ inputs and $k$ outputs, of
size $2^{k/2}\poly(k)$, that on every $z$ with $0\leq z<\pi(2^k-1)$
returns the $(z+1)$st prime.  Its outputs on larger $z$ are unspecified.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Primality of $k$-bit integers is decidable in deterministic time
$\poly(k)$~\cite{AKS04}, and a multitape machine running in time $t$ has
circuits of size $O(t\log t)$~\cite{PF79}.  Hence there is a circuit
$\mathrm{PRIME}_k$ of size $\poly(k)$ deciding primality of $k$-bit
integers.  (Alternatively, Miller--Rabin testing with bases fixed by
Adleman's argument~\cite{Adleman78} gives the same bound nonuniformly.)

Put $h=\ceil{k/2}$, $\Lambda=2^h$ and $J=2^{k-h}$, and split
$\{0,\ldots,2^k-1\}$ into the blocks $[j\Lambda,(j+1)\Lambda)$ for
$0\leq j<J$.  Hardwire the counts $c_j=\pi(j\Lambda-1)$ of primes below
each block, so $c_0=0$.  Fix $z<\pi(2^k-1)$ and let $q$ be the $(z+1)$st
prime, the unique prime with exactly $z$ primes below it.  If
$j\Lambda\leq q$, the primes below $j\Lambda$ are among the $z$ primes below
$q$, so $c_j\leq z$; if $j\Lambda>q$, they include $q$ and the primes below
it, so $c_j\geq z+1$.  Thus $q$ lies in block $j^*$, the largest $j$ with
$c_j\leq z$.

The circuit computes the flags $f_j=[c_j\leq z]$ with $J$ comparisons.
They are monotone, with $f_0=1$, so $g_j=f_j\wedge\neg f_{j+1}$ (with
$f_J=0$) is one exactly at $j^*$.  An OR of masked words selects $j^*$ and
$c_{j^*}$, and a subtraction gives the rank $\rho=z-c_{j^*}$: the number of
primes in block $j^*$ below $q$.  These steps use $O(Jk)$ gates.

For each offset $0\leq i<\Lambda$, a copy of $\mathrm{PRIME}_k$ evaluates
$b_i=[j^*\Lambda+i\text{ is prime}]$; its input is $j^*$ followed by the
constant bits of $i$.  A chain of $(h+1)$-bit incrementers computes
$S_i=\sum_{i'<i}b_{i'}$.  The selector $e_i=b_i\wedge[S_i=\rho]$ is one
exactly at the offset of $q$.  The output has high bits $j^*$ and low bits
the OR of the masked offsets $e_i\wedge i$.  This part costs
$\Lambda\poly(k)+O(\Lambda h)$ gates.  Since $J$ and $\Lambda$ are at most
$2\cdot2^{k/2}$, the total is $2^{k/2}\poly(k)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Explicit hard powers above the square-root
threshold]\label[corollary]{cor:explicit-half}
Let $L\in\mathsf{E}$ and $\varepsilon>1/2$ satisfy
$\CC(L_m)\geq 2^{\varepsilon m}$ for infinitely many $m$.  Then there are
explicit exponents $T_n$ such that, for infinitely many even $n$,
\[
  \CC(P_n^{T_n})\geq 2^{\varepsilon n/2-\varepsilon\log_2 n-O(1)}.
\]
In particular $\CC(P_n^{T_n})\geq 2^{\varepsilon' n}$ infinitely often
for every fixed $\varepsilon'<\varepsilon/2$, and $\varepsilon'$ may be
taken above $1/4$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Pad the input of the circuit of \cref{lem:prime-index}, with $k=k_m$, by
$k_m-m$ constant zero bits.  Every $z\in\bits^m$ satisfies
$z<2^m\leq\pi(2^{k_m}-1)$ by the choice of $k_m$, so this computes $q_m$
and
\[
  \CC(q_m)\leq 2^{k_m/2}\poly(k_m)=2^{m/2}\poly(m),
\]
using $k_m=m+\log_2 m+O(1)$.  Since $\varepsilon>1/2$, this is at most
$2^{\varepsilon m-1}$ for all large $m$.  \Cref{prop:explicit-converse}
then gives explicit $T_n$ with $\CC(P_n^{T_n})\geq2^{\varepsilon m-1}$ for
$n=2k_m$ and infinitely many $m$.  For such $n$,
$m=n/2-\log_2 m-O(1)\geq n/2-\log_2 n-O(1)$, so
$\varepsilon m-1\geq\varepsilon n/2-\varepsilon\log_2 n-O(1)$.
\end{proof}

The full converse remains open only for $\varepsilon\leq1/2$.  The
Impagliazzo--Wigderson hypothesis asserts some fixed $\varepsilon>0$,
possibly small.  Reaching that range by this route needs index-to-prime
circuits of size $2^{\varepsilon m-1}$ for every $\varepsilon>0$, that is
$2^{o(m)}$, which is far below the $2^{m/2}$ of
\cref{lem:prime-index}.  No such circuits are known: uniform deterministic
generation of even one prime of a given length is open~\cite{TCH12}.  The
choice of the $(z+1)$st prime is not the issue.  By
\cref{thm:remainders}, the information in $P_n^T$ is $T\bmod D_B$, which is
determined by the residues of $T$ modulo the prime powers up to $B$, each
carrying at most $k$ bits.  An encoding that places $2^m$ independent bits
in these residues must use about $2^m/k$ distinct prime powers.  A
reduction that queries $r_T$ once per input must then produce suitable
moduli from the index by a small circuit.  Primes in arithmetic
progressions, obtained from Linnik's theorem, lead to the same
index-to-prime problem.  Equivalently, the converse holds if some language
in $\mathsf{E}$ remains exponentially hard when restricted to prime inputs,
since then one may set $T\bmod p$ to that language at every prime
$p\leq B$; but this restates the problem rather than reducing it to the
standard hypothesis.  The Lean development does not formalize uniform
complexity classes, so the results of this subsection are outside its
checked boundary.

\subsection{Short descriptions of hard exponents}\label{sec:exponent-descriptions}

Long binary expansions do not mean that hard exponents lack short
descriptions.  This subsection separates the length of an exponent from
the complexity of describing it, and identifies the resource that
matters.  Its results are prose results; they are outside the Lean
formalization of \cref{sec:lean}.

Fix a universal Turing machine $U$.  For integers $T,n\geq0$, the
conditional Kolmogorov complexity $K(T\mid n)$ is the length of a
shortest program $p$ such that $U$, given $p$ and the binary expansion
of $n$, halts with the binary expansion of $T$ on its output
tape~\cite{LV19}.  A different universal machine changes $K$ by an
additive constant only.

\begin{proposition}[Hard exponents of constant description length]
\label[proposition]{prop:kolmogorov}
There is a constant $c_U$ such that, for all sufficiently large even $n$,
some exponent $T_n^*<D_B$ satisfies
\[
  K(T_n^*\mid n)\leq c_U
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \CC(P_n^{T_n^*})\geq c\,\frac{2^{n/2}}{n},
\]
where $c>0$ is the constant of \cref{thm:typical-powers}.  Consequently
no bound of the form
$\CC(F^T)\leq\beta(n,\CC(F),K(T\mid n))$ with $\beta$ a polynomial
holds, and any valid bound of this form satisfies
$\beta(n,O(n),O(1))=\Omega(2^{n/2}/n)$.  The same holds for the
unconditional complexity, since $K(T_n^*)\leq 2\log_2n+O(1)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Decreasing $c$ if necessary, take $c$ rational.  Let
$\theta_n=\ceil{c2^{n/2}/n}$, and let $T_n^*$ be the least $T\geq0$ with
$\CC(P_n^T)\geq\theta_n$.  By \cref{thm:typical-powers} such a $T$
exists below $D_B$ for all sufficiently large even $n$.  The following
fixed program computes $T_n^*$ from $n$.  For $T=0,1,2,\ldots$ in turn,
compute the truth table of $P_n^T$ by iterating \cref{eq:permutation}
on each of the $2^n$ states.  Then enumerate every circuit with fewer
than $\theta_n$ gates and evaluate it on all $2^n$ inputs.  Output the
first $T$ for which no such circuit agrees with the truth table.  Each
stage is a finite computation, and the search stops at $T_n^*$.  Let
$c_U$ be the length of this program.  For the unconditional bound,
prepend a self-delimiting encoding of $n$.  Since $\CC(P_n)=O(n)$,
these exponents defeat every polynomial $\beta$.
\end{proof}

By \cref{cor:exponent-length}, the exponent $T_n^*$ still has
$\Omega(2^{n/2}/n^2)$ binary digits.  So hard exponents are long but can
be simple.  The upper bound of \cref{thm:main} does not depend on $T$ at
all, so in terms of description complexity the two results match up to
$2^{O(\log n)}$ factors.

The program in the proof is expensive.  It tries up to $D_B$ exponents,
each against $2^{O(\theta_n\log\theta_n)}$ circuits, so it runs in time
$2^{2^{O(n)}}$.  It uses only space $2^{O(n)}$: at any moment it stores
one exponent below $D_B$, one candidate circuit, and one truth table.
We do not claim that a substantially faster short program for a hard
exponent cannot exist.  The next statements show what a cheaper program
would imply.

For a family of exponents $T_n$, a \emph{description in time $t(n)$}
(respectively \emph{in space $S(n)$}) is a family of programs $p_n$ of
length $\poly(n)$ such that $U$, given $p_n$ and $n$, outputs $T_n$
within $t(n)$ steps (respectively, halts using at most $S(n)$ cells of
work tape; the write-only output tape is not charged).  For a family of
maps, membership in $\mathsf{PSPACE}/\poly$ or $\mathsf{EXP}/\poly$
refers to the language of pairs $(x,i)$ such that bit $i$ of the image
of $x$ is one, with polynomial-length advice for each $n$.

\begin{definition}[Residue profile]
For $T\geq0$ and $n\geq1$, the \emph{residue profile}
$\rho_{T,n}:\{0,1,\ldots,2^n\}\to\{0,1,\ldots,2^n\}$ is given by
$\rho_{T,n}(p)=T\bmod p$ for $1\leq p\leq2^n$, and
$\rho_{T,n}(0)=\min(T,2^n)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Residue profiles control powers]
\label[proposition]{prop:residue-profile}
Let $F_n:\bits^n\to\bits^n$ have $\CC(F_n)\leq\poly(n)$, and let
$T_n\geq0$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If the family $\rho_{T_n,n}$ is in $\mathsf{PSPACE}/\poly$, then
  so is the family $F_n^{T_n}$.  The same holds with $\mathsf{EXP}/\poly$
  in place of $\mathsf{PSPACE}/\poly$.
\item For the permutations of \cref{eq:permutation}, the family
  $P_n^{T_n}$ is in $\mathsf{PSPACE}/\poly$ if and only if the family of
  remainder maps $r_{T_n}$ of \cref{thm:remainders} is.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) Write $N=2^n$, $T=T_n$, $F=F_n$, and take as advice the circuit
of $F$ together with the advice for $\rho_{T,n}$.  Given $x$, compute
$y=F^N(x)$ by $N$ evaluations of $F$ with an $(n+1)$-bit counter.  The
state $y$ lies on the cycle reached from $x$.  Its cycle length
$\lambda$ is the least $j\geq1$ with $F^j(y)=y$.  The tail length
$\mu<N$ is the least $i$ with $F^i(x)=F^{i+\lambda}(x)$.  Each of these
searches stores $O(n)$ bits besides one evaluation of $F$.  Read
$m=\rho_{T,n}(0)$.  If $m<\mu$, then $T=m$, and the output is $F^m(x)$.
Otherwise $T\geq\mu$, because $\mu<N$, and
\[
  F^T(x)=F^{\mu+(((T\bmod\lambda)-\mu)\bmod\lambda)}(x),
\]
which uses the value $T\bmod\lambda=\rho_{T,n}(\lambda)$ and at most
$\mu+\lambda\leq2N$ further evaluations.  Each query to $\rho_{T,n}$
is answered by recomputing the requested bits in polynomial space, so
the whole computation runs in polynomial space with polynomial advice.
The computation also runs in time $2^{O(n)}\poly(n)$ plus the cost of
the queries, which gives the $\mathsf{EXP}/\poly$ statement.

(ii) The reductions in the proof of \cref{thm:remainders} are explicit
polynomial-time computations in both directions: $r_T(a)$ is the second
coordinate of $P_n^T(a,0)$, and $P_n^T(a,b)$ is obtained from $a$, $b$,
and $r_T(a)$ by one addition, one comparison, and one selection.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Cheap descriptions give membership in a uniform class]
\label[corollary]{cor:description-resources}
Let $F_n$ and $T_n$ be as in \cref{prop:residue-profile}.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If $T_n$ has a description in space $\poly(n)$, then $F_n^{T_n}$
  is in $\mathsf{PSPACE}/\poly$.  Hence, if
  $\mathsf{PSPACE}\subseteq\mathsf{P}/\poly$, then
  $\CC(F_n^{T_n})\leq\poly(n)$.
\item If $T_n$ has a description in time $2^{\poly(n)}$, then
  $F_n^{T_n}$ is in $\mathsf{EXP}/\poly$.  Hence, if
  $\mathsf{EXP}\subseteq\mathsf{P}/\poly$, then
  $\CC(F_n^{T_n})\leq\poly(n)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
(i) A description in space $S=\poly(n)$ halts within $2^{O(S)}$ steps,
so it emits at most $2^{O(S)}$ output bits.  To compute $T\bmod p$ for
$p\leq 2^n$, rerun the program and fold each output bit into a running
remainder as it appears.  For most-significant-first output, replace
$r$ by $2r+\text{bit}\bmod p$.  For least-significant-first output, also
maintain $2^j\bmod p$.  A running value capped at $2^n$ gives
$\min(T,2^n)$ in the same way: for least-significant-first output, it
suffices to record whether a one appears at position $n$ or later.  This uses $S+O(n)$ cells, so $\rho_{T_n,n}$ is in
$\mathsf{PSPACE}/\poly$ with the program as advice, and
\cref{prop:residue-profile} applies.
(ii) The same streaming computation runs in time $2^{\poly(n)}$.
In both parts, the final implication follows by folding the polynomial
advice into the input: the resulting language lies in $\mathsf{PSPACE}$
(respectively $\mathsf{EXP}$), so it has polynomial-size circuits under
the stated inclusion, and fixing the advice gives circuits for
$F_n^{T_n}$.
\end{proof}

So among short descriptions, space rather than length or time is the
relevant resource.  Constant-length descriptions in space $2^{O(n)}$
reach hard exponents by \cref{prop:kolmogorov}.  Polynomial-space
descriptions reach a hard power only if
$\mathsf{PSPACE}\not\subseteq\mathsf{P}/\poly$.  Time-$2^{\poly(n)}$
descriptions reach one only if
$\mathsf{EXP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{P}/\poly$.

\paragraph{Long but easy exponents.}
Several exponents with exponentially long expansions have residue
profiles in $\mathsf{PSPACE}$, so by \cref{prop:residue-profile} their
powers of any polynomial-size map are in $\mathsf{PSPACE}/\poly$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $T=2^{2^n}$: compute $2^{2^n}\bmod p$ by $2^n$ modular squarings
  with an $n$-bit counter.
\item Power towers $T=2\uparrow\uparrow h$ of any height
  $h<2^{\poly(n)}$, with $h$ given as advice: reduce exponents modulo iterated totients, using
  $a^b\equiv a^{(b\bmod\varphi(p))+\varphi(p)}\pmod p$ for
  $b\geq\log_2p$.  Whenever an intermediate exponent
  $2\uparrow\uparrow j$ is below $n$, it is computed exactly and used
  directly, so the condition is needed only where it holds.
  The iterated totient of $p\leq2^n$ reaches $1$ after
  $O(n)$ steps, so only $O(n)$ levels need explicit work, and each
  totient is found by trial division in polynomial space.
\item $T=D_B-1$: $D_B\bmod p$ is a product of the largest prime powers
  not exceeding $B$, enumerated in polynomial space.
\end{itemize}
The last example is the inversion exponent of $P_n$, so
$P_n^{D_B-1}=P_n^{-1}$.  As noted in \cref{sec:one-way}, this power even
has a linear-size circuit.  Membership in $\mathsf{PSPACE}/\poly$ gives
only an upper bound.  These examples show that neither the length of an
exponent nor its numerical size forces hardness.

\section{Input times and shared evaluation}

\subsection{An iteration count supplied as input}\label{sec:variable}

\begin{definition}
For $m\geq 1$, define
\[
  \Iter_{F,m}:\bits^n\times\bits^m\longrightarrow\bits^n,
  \qquad \Iter_{F,m}(x,t)=F^{\operatorname{val}(t)}(x),
\]
where $\operatorname{val}(t)$ is the nonnegative integer encoded by $t$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Variable-time iteration]\label{thm:variable}
Under the assumptions of \cref{thm:main}, for every $m\geq 1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:variable}
  \CC(\Iter_{F,m})
  =O\!\left(m\left(A+\sqrt{n^3NA}\right)\right).
\end{equation}
In particular,
\[
  \CC(\Iter_{F,n})
  =O\!\left(n\left(A+\sqrt{n^3NA}\right)\right).
\]
If $s$ and $m$ are polynomial in $n$, then
$\CC(\Iter_{F,m})\leq 2^{n/2+O(\log n)}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $t=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}t_j2^j$.  For each $j$, apply
\cref{thm:main} directly to the original map $F$ and the fixed exponent
$2^j$, obtaining a circuit for $G_j=F^{2^j}$.  Starting with $v_0=x$, use
\[
  v_{j+1}=
  \begin{cases}
    G_j(v_j),&t_j=1,\\
    v_j,&t_j=0.
  \end{cases}
\]
Inductively,
$v_j=F^{\sum_{a<j}t_a2^a}(x)$, so $v_m=F^t(x)$.
There are $m$ circuits of the size in \cref{eq:optimized} and $m$
$n$-bit multiplexers.  The extra $O(mn)$ gates are absorbed by the claimed
bound.  Each $G_j$ is constructed from $F$ using the theorem; it is not
obtained by duplicating the circuit for $G_{j-1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Long input times]\label{thm:long-time}
Write $B=A+\sqrt{n^3NA}$.  For every $m\geq1$,
\[
 \CC(\Iter_{F,m})=O\!\left(\min\{mB,\ nB+nm\}\right).
\]
Thus the cost of processing additional time bits is additive once the
state width is fixed.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The first bound is \cref{thm:variable}.  For the second, compute the exact
eventual cycle length $\ell=\ell(x)$ in $O(B)$ gates using
\cref{thm:labels}.  Replace the input time by
\[
 u=\begin{cases}
 t,&t<N,\\
 N+(t-N)\bmod\ell,&t\geq N.
 \end{cases}
\]
Then $u<2N$ and $F^u(x)=F^t(x)$: in the second case both times are past
cycle entry and have the same residue modulo $\ell$.

Compute $t\bmod\ell$ by streaming its bits from most significant to least
significant, retaining an $O(n)$-bit remainder.  Each step doubles the
remainder, adds one bit, and conditionally subtracts $\ell$; a spare bit
prevents overflow.  Shared-carry addition and subtraction give $O(n)$
gates per input bit, including comparison and selection.  The bounded
modular correction by $N$ and the final selection cost $O(n^3+m)$ with
the arithmetic circuits already used in the proof.  Hence forming $u$
costs $O(nm+n^3)$, and its $n+1$ bits feed \cref{thm:variable} at cost
$O(nB)$.  Since $n^3\leq B$, the second bound follows.  If $m\leq n$,
one may simply use the original $m$-stage construction.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Sharing a dictionary among independent trajectories}
\label{sec:batch}

Let
\[
  B_{F,T,r}(x_1,\ldots,x_r)
   =\bigl(F^T(x_1),\ldots,F^T(x_r)\bigr).
\]
The inputs $x_1,\ldots,x_r$ are independent $n$-bit words.  The following
construction uses one checkpoint dictionary for all of them.

\begin{theorem}[Batched fixed powers]\label{thm:batch}
Under the assumptions of \cref{thm:main}, for every $1\leq r\leq N$,
$T\geq 0$, and $1\leq L\leq N$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:batch-tradeoff}
  \CC(B_{F,T,r})
   =O\!\left(rLs+n^3\left(rL+\frac{N}{L}\right)\right).
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:batch-opt}
  \CC(B_{F,T,r})
   =O\!\left(rA+\sqrt{rn^3NA}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Set $K=2L$.  If $T<K$, unroll $T$ copies of $F$ independently for each
input.  Otherwise use the checkpoint set and dictionary from
\cref{sec:fixed}.  Generate the first $K+1$ states of all $r$ trajectories.
Together with one copy of the dictionary, these give
\[
  R=|H|+r(K+1)=O(N/L+rL)
\]
records.  Attach the trajectory number and within-trajectory position to
each query.  Because $r\leq N$ and $L\leq N$, the record width and
$\log(R+2)$ are $O(n)$.  One application of \cref{lem:lookup} therefore
resolves all queries in $O(n^3(N/L+rL))$ gates and returns them in their
original order.

Select a matching checkpoint separately within each trajectory.  Run
repeated-state detection separately on each trajectory as well; this
costs $O(rn^3L)$ gates and does not confuse a collision between two
different trajectories with a repetition along one trajectory.
The correction trajectory and output selection are also performed
separately for each input.  The fixed residue table may be implemented
with a separate $O(nL)$-gate lookup for each trajectory, whose total
cost is absorbed by $O(rn^3L)$.  The two simulation phases together cost
$O(rLs)$ gates.  This proves \cref{eq:batch-tradeoff}.

\begin{samepage}
Choose
\[
  L=\max\left\{1,\ceil{\sqrt{n^3N/(rA)}}\right\}.
\]
\end{samepage}
As before $1\leq L\leq N$.  If $rA\leq n^3N$, then
$rAL=O(\sqrt{rn^3NA})$ and $n^3N/L=O(\sqrt{rn^3NA})$.
If $rA>n^3N$, the choice is $L=1$ and the cost is $O(rA)$.
This gives \cref{eq:batch-opt}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Independent input times]\label[corollary]{cor:batch-variable}
For $1\leq r\leq N$ and $m\geq 1$, the map
\[
  \bigl((x_1,t_1),\ldots,(x_r,t_r)\bigr)
   \longmapsto
  \bigl(F^{t_1}(x_1),\ldots,F^{t_r}(x_r)\bigr),
  \qquad t_i\in\bits^m,
\]
has a circuit of size
\begin{equation}\label{eq:batch-variable}
  O\!\left(m\left(rA+\sqrt{rn^3NA}\right)\right).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Use the binary-power construction of \cref{thm:variable}.  At stage $j$,
apply \cref{thm:batch} with update map $F$ and exponent $2^j$ on the $r$
current states, then use the $j$th bit of each $t_i$ to select whether to
retain or update that state.  The $O(mrn)$ selection gates are absorbed
by \cref{eq:batch-variable}.
\end{proof}

For polynomial $s$ and $m$, and $r=\floor{2^{\alpha n}}$ with fixed
$0\leq\alpha\leq 1$, \cref{eq:batch-variable} is at most
\[
  2^{(1+\alpha)n/2+O(\log n)}.
\]
This is an upper-bound sharing statement.  It is not a direct-sum
separation against optimal circuits for a single trajectory.

\subsection{A polynomial improvement with AKS sorting networks}
\label{sec:aks}

The factor $n^3$ in \cref{thm:main} has two sources: $O(n)$ gates per
comparator on $O(n)$-bit records, and the $O(\log^2 R)=O(n^2)$ comparators
per record of Batcher's networks.  Ajtai, Koml\'os, and Szemer\'edi
constructed comparator networks sorting $R$ keys with $O(R\log R)$
comparators~\cite{AKS83}.  Replacing Batcher's networks by theirs saves
one factor of $n$.  Their constants are very large, so this is an
asymptotic statement only.  The Lean development mechanizes the Batcher
version; nothing in this subsection is mechanized.

\begin{lemma}[Lookup and repetition with AKS networks]\label[lemma]{lem:lookup-aks}
The dictionary lookup of \cref{lem:lookup} and the repeated-state detection
of \cref{lem:repeat} have circuits of sizes
\[
  O\!\left((M+Q)\bigl(w+\log(M+Q+2)\bigr)\log(M+Q+2)\right)
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  O\!\left(Q\bigl(w+\log(Q+2)\bigr)\log(Q+2)\right),
\]
respectively.  In particular, for $w=O(n)$ and $\log(M+Q+2)=O(n)$ both
costs are $O(n^2(M+Q))$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Repeat the proofs of \cref{lem:lookup,lem:repeat} with every sorting
network replaced by an AKS network on the same records, padded with
dummy records as before.  The networks use $O(R\log(R+2))$ comparators,
each of cost $O(w+\log(R+2))$.  The scans and the selection of a
flagged record cost $O(R(w+\log(R+2)))$ gates and are unchanged.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[AKS-strengthened tradeoff]\label{thm:aks}
Under the assumptions of \cref{thm:main}, put $A_2=s+n^2$.  For every
integer $T\geq0$ and every integer $1\leq L\leq N$,
\[
  \CC(F^T)=O\!\left(Ls+n^2\left(L+\frac NL\right)\right),
  \qquad\text{hence}\qquad
  \CC(F^T)=O\!\left(A_2+n\sqrt{NA_2}\right).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Use the construction of \cref{sec:fixed} unchanged, and implement its
lookup and repeated-state detection by \cref{lem:lookup-aks}.  These now
cost $O(n^2(N/L+L))$ and $O(n^2L)$ gates.  Every other component already
fits this bound: the two simulations cost $O(Ls)$; the residue-table
multiplexer, the trajectory selections, and the final output selection
cost $O(nL)$; and the long division and modular correction of
\cref{eq:period} cost $O(n^2)$.  For the optimized bound, choose
$L=\max\{1,\ceil{\sqrt{n^2N/A_2}}\}$ and argue as for
\cref{eq:L-choice}, with $n^3$ replaced by $n^2$ and $A$ by $A_2$.
Then $L\leq\ceil{\sqrt N}\leq N$ since $A_2\geq n^2$, and
$\sqrt{n^2NA_2}=n\sqrt{NA_2}$.
\end{proof}

The proofs of the following results use the lookup and repetition
circuits only through the per-record cost of \cref{lem:lookup,lem:repeat},
together with $O(Ls)$ simulation, $O(nL)$ selection, and $O(n^2)$
arithmetic.  Each therefore holds with $n^3$ replaced by $n^2$ in its
parameterized bound and with $A$ replaced by $A_2$ in its optimized bound:
the long-trajectory, invariant-set and image-sensitive bounds
(\cref{thm:active-mass,thm:invariant,cor:image}), exact permutation
inversion (\cref{cor:inverse}), variable-time iteration
(\cref{thm:variable}, by $m$ applications of \cref{thm:aks}), and batched
fixed powers and independent input times
(\cref{thm:batch,cor:batch-variable}).  For the batched bound, the shared
lookup costs $O(n^2(N/L+rL))$ and the $r$ separate repetition tests cost
$O(rn^2L)$, giving
\[
  \CC(B_{F,T,r})=O\!\left(rA_2+n\sqrt{rNA_2}\right).
\]
For the permutation of \cref{sec:lower}, $\CC(P_n)=O(n)$ gives $A_2=O(n^2)$
and $\max_T\CC(P_n^T)=O(n^22^{n/2})$, within a factor $O(n^3)$ of the
lower bound in \cref{thm:lower}.  We do not claim the improvement for the
navigation and target results of \cref{sec:structure} or for
\cref{thm:long-time}, whose proofs contain further $n^3$ terms that we
have not re-examined.

\section{Functional-graph navigation}\label{sec:structure}

Write $B=A+\sqrt{n^3NA}$.  For a state $x$, let $\mu(x)$ be its distance
to its eventual cycle, let $\ell(x)$ be that cycle's length, and let $c(x)$
be the lexicographically least state on the cycle.  Define
$\phi(x)\in\{0,\ldots,\ell(x)-1\}$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phase}
 F^t(x)=F^{(\phi(x)+t)\bmod\ell(x)}(c(x))\qquad(t\geq\mu(x)).
\end{equation}
The phase is an eventual-orbit label; it does not assert that a transient
state itself lies on the cycle.  These labels are unique.  Cycle lengths
are encoded with $n+1$ bits, since a cycle may have length $N$.

\begin{theorem}[Cycle labels]\label{thm:labels}
A circuit of size $O(B)$ computes $(\ell(x),c(x),\phi(x))$ for every $x$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Fix $L$ and use the checkpoint construction with $K=2L$.  Hardwire the
three labels for each checkpoint.  If $F^d(x)=h$ for $d\leq K$, output
\[
 \ell(x)=\ell(h),\qquad c(x)=c(h),\qquad
 \phi(x)=(\phi(h)-d)\bmod\ell(h).
\]
This follows by applying \cref{eq:phase} after both transients have ended.
All records still have $O(n)$ bits.

If there is no checkpoint hit, the prefix repeats.  Sort prefix records
by state and then by original index.  Among adjacent equal-state pairs,
choose the pair with smallest second index $j$, retaining its first
index $i$.  This is the first repetition in the original trajectory:
any earliest second occurrence is adjacent to its first occurrence in
its equal-state run.  The states before $j$ are distinct, so
$i=\mu(x)$ and $[i,j)$ consists of exactly one cycle traversal.
Set $\ell=j-i$, and select the minimum state $c=x_p$ over $i\leq p<j$.
Then $\phi(x)=(-p)\bmod\ell$.  Minimum selection and the interval mask
cost $O(nK)$ gates, and word arithmetic costs $O(n^2)$ gates.
Sorting and lookup cost $O(n^3(L+N/L))$, while the prefix costs $O(Ls)$.
Optimizing $L$ proves the claim.  Discarded branches use safe positive
dummy moduli, as in the fixed-power construction.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Transient depth]\label{thm:depth}
The exact transient depth $\mu(x)$ is computable by a circuit of size $O(B)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $D=N!$ and $R=F^D$.  Every trajectory is cyclic after $N$ steps,
every cycle length divides $D$, and $D\geq N$.  Thus $R$ maps into the
cyclic states, fixes each such state, and satisfies $R^2=R$.  It commutes
with all iterates of $F$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:projection-depth}
 F^i(x)=F^i(R(x))\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad i\geq\mu(x).
\end{equation}
Indeed, equality is equivalent to $F^i(x)=R(F^i(x))$, which is exactly
cyclicity.  The projection costs $O(B)$ gates by \cref{thm:main}.

Fix a checkpoint parameter $L$ and set $K=2L$.  Retain the first $K+1$
states of both trajectories and compare corresponding states.  If the
states at position $K$ agree, select the first equality; its index is
$\mu(x)$.  Otherwise $\mu(x)>K$, so the original prefix has no repetition
and must meet a checkpoint $h=F^d(x)$ with $d<K$.  Every such hit is
transient and satisfies $\mu(x)=d+\mu(h)$.

Hardwire $\mu(h)$ with each checkpoint and resolve all prefix queries
using \cref{lem:lookup}.  Either branch uses $O(n)$-bit records; comparing,
selecting, and adding indices cost $O(nL)$ gates.  The two prefixes and
shared lookup cost $O(Ls+n^3(L+N/L))$.  Use the same $L$ for the projection
and these operations, then optimize as in \cref{eq:L-choice}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Compressed checkpoint forest]\label[lemma]{lem:checkpoint-forest}
Let $H$ be the checkpoint set from \cref{lem:checkpoints}.  Cut the outgoing
edge of every cyclic vertex, and let $S$ be the union of the paths from
$H$ to their first cyclic vertices, including both endpoints.  There is
a set $J$ with $H\subseteq J\subseteq S$ and $|J|\leq2|H|$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every root and every branching vertex of the forest on $S$ lies in $J$.
\item From each nonroot $a\in J$, the next vertex of $J$ is reached after
some $1\leq d_a\leq2L$ steps.  The segments
$a,F(a),\ldots,F^{d_a-1}(a)$ are pairwise disjoint.
\item Each $a\in J$ has labels $p(a),q(a)<N$ such that, for $a,h\in J$,
\[
 p(a)\leq p(h)\leq q(a)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 F^t(h)=a\text{ for some }0\leq t\leq\mu(h).
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Take $J$ to be $H$ together with the roots and branching vertices of $S$.
Every leaf belongs to $H$: any other vertex on a checkpoint path has a
predecessor on that path.  In a rooted forest, the number of roots plus
the number of vertices with at least two children is at most the number
of leaves.  Indeed, summing the numbers of children gives the number of
nonroot vertices; a branching vertex contributes at least two.  Therefore
$|J|\leq |H|+|\text{leaves}|\leq2|H|$.

Apply checkpoint coverage to $F(a)$.  A checkpoint hit gives a positive
hit from $a$ within $2L$ steps; a repetition gives cycle entry within $L$
steps, where a root is marked.  Hence the next marked vertex is at
distance at most $2L$.  Between marks, each vertex of $S$ has a unique
predecessor in $S$, so two such segments cannot merge.  Finally order
vertices by a preorder traversal of the cut forest.  Descendants of a
vertex occupy a contiguous interval; its first and last ranks give
$p,q$.  Only the labels of $J$ need to be stored.  The Lean proof obtains
the same interval property by lexicographically ordering root-to-vertex
paths, without formalizing a traversal algorithm.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Reachability and first hitting times]\label{thm:reachability}
A circuit of size $O(B)$, on input $(x,y)$, decides whether
$F^t(x)=y$ for some $t\geq0$ and returns the least such $t$ when it exists.
The output may use a separate existence flag and $n$ time bits.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Use a common parameter $L$ in the depth and cycle-label constructions.
Write $K=2L$ and construct the forest of \cref{lem:checkpoint-forest}.
Store a dictionary of the marked vertices $a\in J$, with their states,
depths, and interval labels.  A second dictionary has one key
$F^{d_a-1}(a)$ for each nonroot mark $a$, with the same payload for $a$.
These last-predecessor keys are distinct by disjointness of the segments.
Both dictionaries have $O(N/L)$ records of $O(n)$ bits.

Suppose first that $y$ is transient.  Generate the prefixes of $x$ and
$y$ through time $K$.  If $y$ occurs in the prefix of $x$, accept.
Otherwise select any marked vertex $h$ in that prefix; if none exists,
reject.  To select a candidate ancestor $a$, use $a=y$ if $y\in J$.
Otherwise query the second dictionary on the prefix of $y$, retain only
matches with $\mu(a)\geq\mu(y)$, and select any retained record.  Reject
if there is no such record.  Finally accept exactly when
\[
 y\in\{a,F(a),\ldots,F^K(a)\}
 \quad\hbox{and}\quad p(a)\leq p(h)\leq q(a).
\]
The first test uses a third short prefix.  It is essential for rejecting
side branches outside $S$.

Every acceptance is sound: $x$ reaches $h$, the interval test says that
$h$ reaches $a$, and the verification prefix reaches $y$.  Conversely,
suppose $x$ reaches a transient $y$ beyond its inspected prefix.
That prefix cannot repeat and must contain a checkpoint.  Every selected
mark $h$ precedes $y$, so $y\in S$.  If $y$ is unmarked, it lies in a
unique compressed segment, whose last-predecessor key appears within
$K$ steps of $y$.  Its lower endpoint passes the depth filter.
Moreover, every retained key has the same property: if
$F^j(y)=F^{d_a-1}(a)$ and $\mu(a)\geq\mu(y)$, decreasing depth gives
$j\leq d_a-1$.  Unique predecessors inside the segment then give
$y=F^{d_a-1-j}(a)$.  A marked $h$ can reach this segment only through
$a$.  Thus the selected record passes both final tests.  The marked-target
case takes $a=y$ directly.

Three prefixes cost $O(Ls)$ gates.  Shared lookup costs
$O(n^3(L+N/L))$, and depth filtering, record selection, prefix equality,
and interval comparisons add $O(nL+n^3)$ gates.  Depths and cycle labels
fit the same tradeoff.  Optimization therefore gives $O(B)$ gates.
For a positive transient query, the first hitting time is simply
$\mu(x)-\mu(y)$, since depth decreases by one on each transient step.

If $y$ is cyclic, it is reachable exactly when $c(x)=c(y)$.  On a positive
instance its first hitting time is
\[
 \mu(x)+\bigl((\phi(y)-\phi(x)-\mu(x))\bmod\ell(x)\bigr),
\]
where the parenthesized residue lies in $[0,\ell(x)-1]$.  This is the
first time on or after entry into the cycle with the required phase.
Finally $\mu(x)+\ell(x)\leq N$, so every first hitting time is at most
$N-1$.  Arithmetic may use $n+1$ intermediate bits.  The time field is unspecified on negative instances; an additional
$O(n)$-gate mask can set it to zero if desired.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Complete visit schedules]\label[corollary]{cor:schedules}
A circuit of size $O(B)$, on input $(x,y)$, returns a success flag and
two integers $d,p$ describing every visit time.  A zero flag denotes no
visits.  For a positive flag, $d<N$ is the first visit, $0\leq p\leq N$,
and
\[
 \{t\geq0:F^t(x)=y\}=\{d+kp:k\geq0\}.
\]
Here $p=0$ for transient $y$, giving the singleton $\{d\}$, and
$p=\ell(y)$ for cyclic $y$.  The period field uses $n+1$ bits.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Use \cref{thm:reachability} for the flag and $d$, and \cref{thm:labels}
and an $O(B)$ cyclicity test for $p$.  After the first visit, a later
time $t\geq d$ is a visit exactly when $F^{t-d}(y)=y$.  Return times of a
cyclic point are precisely the multiples of its least positive period.
A transient point has no positive return.  This proves the descriptor.
\end{proof}

For example, a visit occurs by deadline $T$ exactly when the flag is
positive and $d\leq T$.  The number of visits at times $0,\ldots,T$ is
\[
 \begin{cases}
 0,&\text{flag zero or }T<d,\\
 1,&\text{flag positive, }T\geq d,\ p=0,\\
 1+\floor{(T-d)/p},&\text{flag positive, }T\geq d,\ p>0.
 \end{cases}
\]
These are arithmetic consequences of the returned descriptor; the
descriptor bound does not include a circuit for processing an additional
deadline input of unspecified length.

\paragraph{Relation to navigation data structures.}
Function powers and path queries have established data-structure
formulations~\cite{MRRR12,GHN19}.  In particular, nearest labeled
successors include reachability of a fixed singleton target.
Those results charge representation space and query time in a RAM model.
Here the given object is a succinct transition circuit, and every gate
accessing a nonuniform table is charged.  These differences specify the
claim being compared; they do not establish historical novelty of the
present bounds.

\subsection{Recognizable targets and terminating computations}

\begin{corollary}[First recognizable target]\label[corollary]{cor:target}
Let $P:\bits^n\to\bits$ have circuit size $a$, and put
$A_P=s+a+n^3$.  Eventual target membership,
\[
 H(x)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \exists t\geq0:\ P(F^t(x))=1,
\]
has a circuit of size $O(A_P+\sqrt{n^3NA_P})$.  Within the same bound,
a circuit returns the first visited target state and its exact first
visit time on positive instances.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Set $G(x)=x$ when $P(x)=1$ and $G(x)=F(x)$ otherwise.  This costs
$O(s+a+n)$ gates.  A first target, if one exists, is visited before
step $N$, since an earlier repeated state would preclude a later first
visit.  Hence $G^N(x)$ is the first target state on positive instances,
and $H(x)=P(G^N(x))$.  Apply \cref{thm:main} to $G$.
For the time, apply \cref{thm:depth} to $G$.  On a positive instance,
the first target is an absorbing fixed point.  A cyclic state cannot
precede it on the trajectory: iteration is injective on cyclic states,
so any cyclic predecessor of that fixed point is the same point.
Consequently $\mu_G(x)$ is exactly the first target time.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Complete-configuration simulation]\label[corollary]{cor:halting}
Suppose a deterministic computation has its entire configuration encoded
in $b$ bits and a next-configuration circuit of size $s$.  If halting
configurations are absorbing and every valid initial configuration
halts, its final-configuration map on valid initial states has circuit
size
\[
 O\!\left(s+b^3+\sqrt{b^3 2^b(s+b^3)}\right).
\]
The exact first halting time can also be returned within this bound.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Before halting, no configuration can repeat.  The iterate at time $2^b$
is therefore the final configuration.  Apply \cref{thm:main} with
state width $b$.  The first halt is the transient depth, by the same
absorbing-state argument as in \cref{cor:target}; use \cref{thm:depth}.
Initialization and output decoding circuits are
charged separately.
\end{proof}
In particular, when $s=\poly(b)$, both the final configuration and the
first halting time have circuit size
\[
  2^{b/2}\poly(b).
\]
For a nearly in-place computation on an $n$-bit input, with complete state
width $b=n+O(\log n)$ and a polynomial-size transition, this specializes to
$2^{n/2}\poly(n)$.  Polynomial-size initialization and output decoding
preserve that bound.  The parameter $b$ includes all persistent input
information, control, and workspace; these bits are counted even when an
abstract machine model calls part of its storage read-only.  A general
polynomial-space machine can have far more than $n+O(\log n)$ configuration
bits, so the corollary gives no such input-length bound for all of PSPACE.

\subsection{Synchronous and recurring queries}\label{sec:eventual-queries}

The projection $R=F^{N!}$ from the proof of \cref{thm:depth} also supplies
queries about the eventual dynamics.

\begin{corollary}[Eventual synchronous coalescence]\label[corollary]{cor:coalescence}
A circuit of size $O(B)$ decides, on input $(x,y)$, whether there exists
$t\geq0$ such that $F^t(x)=F^t(y)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The required equivalence is
\[
 \exists t\geq0:\ F^t(x)=F^t(y)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F^N(x)=F^N(y).
\]
Equality before time $N$ persists to time $N$.  Both states are cyclic
by that time, and $F$ restricts to a bijection on the cyclic states.
Therefore later equality can be canceled back to time $N$.  Compute
two copies of $F^N$ and compare their outputs.
\end{proof}

This query requires the same elapsed time on both trajectories.  Orbit
intersection permits different elapsed times and is a different predicate;
for example, distinct points of one cycle never coalesce synchronously.

\begin{corollary}[Recurring and permanent targets]\label[corollary]{cor:recurring}
For a fixed predicate $P:\bits^n\to\bits$ of circuit size $a$, each of
\[
 \forall u\ \exists t\geq u:\ P(F^t(x))=1,
 \qquad
 \exists u\ \forall t\geq u:\ P(F^t(x))=1
\]
is decidable by a circuit of size
$O(A_P+\sqrt{n^3NA_P})$, where $A_P=s+a+n^3$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
A target recurs arbitrarily late exactly when the eventual cycle contains
an accepting state.  Start at $R(x)$ and use the absorbing-target
construction in \cref{cor:target}.  If $G$ makes accepting states
absorbing, the circuit outputs $P(G^N(R(x)))$.  A successful visit is
periodic and hence recurs arbitrarily late; every sufficiently late visit
from $x$ is a future visit from $R(x)$.  The projection and the target
circuit each obey the stated bound.  Permanent eventual acceptance is
the complement of recurrence of the predicate $1-P$, which changes the
bound only by a constant number of gates.
\end{proof}

\section{Structured transitions}\label{sec:structured}

The universal bound uses only the state width and the size of one
transition.  Additional structure can give compact circuits for whole
iterates even when an orbit visits exponentially many states.  Affine
payload updates stay affine along each autonomous controller trajectory;
sparse counter edits give orbit coordinates described by a few intervals.
In both cases, selecting and evaluating the resulting description has a
charged circuit cost that can improve on the universal bound.

\subsection{Small controllers with affine payloads}\label{sec:controller}

The next result isolates all exponential cost in the autonomous controller,
allowing a much larger affine payload.

\begin{theorem}[Affine-controller powering]\label{thm:affine-controller}
Let $q+p=n\geq1$, and write a state as $(u,v)\in\bits^q\times\mathbb F_2^p$.
Suppose
\[
 F(u,v)=\bigl(g(u),A(u)v+b(u)\bigr),
\]
where $g:\bits^q\to\bits^q$, $A(u)$ is a $p\times p$ binary matrix,
and $b(u)\in\mathbb F_2^p$.  For every fixed integer $T\geq0$,
\[
 \CC(F^T)=O\!\left(2^q(q+p^2)\right).
\]
For an $m$-bit input exponent, the size bound is
$O(m2^q(q+p^2))$.  In particular, $q=O(\log n)$ gives polynomial-size
circuits for every fixed power, uniformly in the magnitude of $T$.
The matrices may be singular and need not commute.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Once the initial controller $u$ is fixed, the controller trajectory and
all affine maps applied to $v$ are fixed.  Composition of affine maps is
affine, so for some hardwired matrix $M_T(u)$ and vector $d_T(u)$,
\[
 F^T(u,v)=\bigl(g^T(u),M_T(u)v+d_T(u)\bigr).
\]
For each of the $2^q$ controller values, evaluate its fixed affine map
using $O(p^2)$ gates and provide its fixed controller output.  Compute
each controller-equality flag once, and share it across the $q+p$ output
bits.  A scan selects the matching record using
$O(2^q(q+p))$ additional gates.  This proves the fixed-power bound,
including a charge for every table-selection gate.

For an input exponent, conditionally apply the circuits for $F^{2^j}$
to the current full state, as in \cref{sec:variable}.  Each stage updates
the controller as well as the payload and adds $O(q+p)$ selection gates.
Summing over $m$ stages gives the second bound.
\end{proof}

Autonomy is essential to this argument: if the controller update depends
on $v$, its future choices need not be determined by the initial $u$.
No efficient algorithm for obtaining $M_T(u)$ from a succinct transition
is asserted here.  The bound uses ordinary affine composition and finite
selection; it does not claim a new algebraic closure principle.

The same reasoning applies beyond affine maps.  Let $\mathcal M$ be a
family of maps on $p$-bit payloads containing the identity and closed under
composition, and suppose \emph{every} member has a circuit of size at
most $\kappa$.  If the autonomous controller selects maps from
$\mathcal M$, every fixed power costs
\[
 O\!\left(2^q(\kappa+q+p+1)\right),
\]
and an $m$-bit input time multiplies this by $m$.  Closure alone is insufficient: the uniform evaluation
bound on composite maps is a separate hypothesis.  For nonuniform circuit
existence, no efficient procedure for computing those composites is needed.

\subsection{Sparse modifications of a counter}\label{sec:sparse-counter}

The state-space parameter is not the only source of compression.  Identify
$\bits^n$ with $\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z$, where $N=2^n$, and write
$R(x)=x+1\pmod N$.  A permutation obtained by changing a small number of
counter successors admits a bound linear in its sparse description, even
when it is a single cycle through all $N$ states.

\begin{theorem}[Sparse-counter powers]\label{thm:sparse-counter}
Let $n\geq1$, let $F$ be a permutation of $\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z$, and let
$r=|\{x:F(x)\ne R(x)\}|$.  For every fixed $T\in\mathbb Z$,
\[
 \CC(F^T)=O\bigl(n(r+1)\bigr).
\]
For an unsigned $m$-bit time supplied as input,
\[
 \CC\bigl((x,t)\mapsto F^t(x)\bigr)=O\bigl(n(r+m+1)\bigr).
\]
Both constants are absolute.  The dependence on time-input length is additive.
\end{theorem}

\subsubsection{Commutation defects and displacement}

The fixed-power assertion follows from a more general statement.

\begin{proposition}[Sparse-counter centralizers]\label[proposition]{prop:centralizer}
Let $F$ be any map on $\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z$ differing from $R$ at $r$
inputs.  Every permutation $H$ commuting with $F$ has a displacement
function $\delta_H(x)=H(x)-x\pmod N$ that changes on at most $2r$ counter
edges.  Consequently, $\CC(H)=O(n(r+1))$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put $E=\{x:F(x)\ne R(x)\}$.  If $x\notin E$ and $H(x)\notin E$, then
\[
 H(R(x))=H(F(x))=F(H(x))=R(H(x)).
\]
Thus the set of commutation failures for $H$ and $R$ lies in
$E\cup H^{-1}(E)$ and has size at most $2r$.  Commutation at $x$ is
exactly $\delta_H(x+1)=\delta_H(x)$.  Cutting the ordinary linear order
$0,\ldots,N-1$ at these changes gives at most $2r+1$ constant intervals.
Compare the input with their endpoints, select a hardwired displacement,
and add it modulo $N$, at cost $O(n(r+1))$.

An alternative implementation, used in Lean, avoids ordering the changes.
Let $D=\{i:\delta_H(i+1)\ne\delta_H(i)\}$, with cyclic addition in the
arguments and ordinary integer comparisons below.  Telescoping gives
\[
 H(x)=x+\delta_H(0)+
       \sum_{\substack{i\in D\\i<x}}
         \bigl(\delta_H(i+1)-\delta_H(i)\bigr)\pmod N.
\]
Each summand uses one comparison, a conditional constant word, and a
linear-size adder.  The wraparound edge is never selected.  All gates,
including the shared carry chains, are charged.
\end{proof}

Every signed power of a permutation commutes with it, proving the first
part of \cref{thm:sparse-counter}.  The proposition only requires
injectivity of $H$ in its cardinality argument; it does not require $F$
to be a permutation.  Its set-containment argument works on arbitrary
universes, before finiteness or coordinates are imposed.

The geometry relating commutation failures to blocks in cycles is
established permutation theory.  Moreno and Rivera characterize these
blocks in their Theorem~3.8 and Corollary~3.10~\cite{MR14}.  Here we use
the elementary defect containment and account for the Boolean cost of the
resulting representation.  Historical priority of these precise circuit
bounds has not been determined.

\subsubsection{A chart constructed from the edited successors}

For the live-time bound, assume first $r>0$ and cut the counter cycle
immediately after each point of $E$.  There are $r$ nonempty circular
arcs.  Inside an arc $F$ increments; its only possible change is at the
last state.  Since $F$ and $R$ are permutations agreeing off $E$,
\[
 F(E)=R(E).
\]
Indeed, the images of the unedited inputs coincide, so their complements
coincide.  Therefore each edited edge leads to an arc start, and these
edges induce a permutation of the $r$ arcs.

Decompose that permutation into cycles and concatenate the arcs in each
cycle, retaining their individual lengths.  Assign to a state the triple
$(c,p,\ell)$ consisting of its cycle identifier, its position within that
cycle, and its total cycle length.  The coordinate map and its inverse
are piecewise translations on the original arcs and their concatenated
images.  At most one original arc crosses zero; splitting it there adds
one linear interval.  Comparisons, shared record selection, and addition
therefore implement each direction in $O(n(r+1))$ gates.  Identifiers and
positions use $n$ bits, while lengths use $n+1$ bits to allow $\ell=N$.
All intermediate arithmetic uses $O(n)$ bits.

In these coordinates one step preserves $c,\ell$ and increments $p$
modulo $\ell$.  For an $m$-bit input $t$, compute its remainder by scanning
bits from most significant to least significant.  With current remainder
$u<\ell$, the next remainder is $2u+b$ or $2u+b-\ell$, according as
$2u+b<\ell$ or not.  A comparison and subtraction cost $O(n)$ per bit;
one extra bit prevents overflow.  Add $p$, reduce once more, and decode.
The cost is $O(nr+nm+n)$.  If $r=0$, ordinary addition modulo $N$ gives
the same bound.  This proves the second part of \cref{thm:sparse-counter}.
For fixed signed $T$, hardwire $T\bmod\ell$ for each cycle instead.

\begin{corollary}[Labels and navigation for sparse counters]\label[corollary]{cor:sparse-labels}
For a permutation differing from the counter at $r$ states, the cycle
length, numerically least cycle vertex, and phase of an input state can be computed
with $O(n(r+1))$ gates.  The same bound suffices, on input $(x,y)$, for
reachability, the exact first hitting time, and a descriptor of all visits.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Cut the ordinary linear order after each exception, obtaining at most
$r+1$ intervals.  Within each interval $[a,b)$, the trajectory follows
ordinary addition.  Hardwire its cycle representative $c$, cycle length
$\ell$, and phase $p_a$ at its first state.  An input $x$ in that interval
has label $(c,\ell,(p_a+x-a)\bmod\ell)$.  The interval length is at most
$\ell$, so one addition and conditional subtraction suffice for the phase.
Comparisons and record selection cost $O(n)$ per interval.

Compute the two labels.  Since every state is cyclic, $y$ is reachable
from $x$ exactly when $c(x)=c(y)$.  On a positive instance, the first hit is
\[
 d=(\phi(y)-\phi(x))\bmod\ell(x),
\]
and all visits occur at times $d+k\ell(x)$, $k\geq0$.  Both phases are
already below the common cycle length, so the difference needs only
comparison and subtraction: use $\phi(y)-\phi(x)$ when nonnegative, and
$\ell(x)-(\phi(x)-\phi(y))$ otherwise.  This costs $O(n)$ additional gates.
The descriptor also determines the exact bounded visit count; this is an
arithmetic interpretation of its output, with no deadline input charged.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Polynomial-time construction from an edit list]
\label[proposition]{prop:sparse-compiler}
Given $n$ and an explicit list of the $r$ edited successors of the
$n$-bit counter, one can check permutation preservation and construct an
orbit chart of $O(r+1)$ records, each of $O(n)$ bits, in time polynomial
in $n$ and $r+1$.  For a valid list, the chart supplies the encoding and
decoding used above.  Given also a signed binary integer $T$, one can
construct a piecewise-translation table for $F^T$ with at most $2r+1$
linear intervals in time polynomial in $n$, $r+1$, and $\log(|T|+2)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Compare the edited outputs with the freed counter outputs to check
$F(E)=R(E)$, after checking distinct input keys.  Sort the cuts, compute
arc lengths, follow the induced permutation of the arcs, and form prefix
sums on each of its cycles.  These operations construct the chart using
arithmetic on $O(n)$-bit integers.  The case $r=0$ is the ordinary counter
chart.  For a signed power, reduce $T$ modulo each chart cycle length.
Use the chart to evaluate $F^{-T}$ on $E$, and test the $O(r)$ candidate
change edges $E\cup F^{-T}(E)$ from \cref{prop:centralizer}.  Sorting their
successors and evaluating the displacement at the interval starts gives
the table; discard boundaries without a displacement change.
\end{proof}

The input here is the explicit edit list, whose entries specify where
counter edges have changed and their new successors.  Given only a circuit
for $F$, identifying that list is a separate task: the proposition asserts
no polynomial-time extraction of exceptions from a transition circuit.
The chart construction avoids enumeration of the $N=2^n$ states.
The explicit arithmetic and selection constructions above then compile
an $m$-bit iterator in time polynomial in $n,r+1,m$ with the gate bound
of \cref{thm:sparse-counter}.

The executable \texttt{sparse\_counter.py} implements this chart and interval
compiler.  Independent finite checks and their scope are recorded in
\texttt{REVIEW.md}; they do not prove the gate bound or compiler correctness.

\subsubsection{Large transitions and charged coordinate changes}

The improvement can apply to transitions with genuinely exponential
minimum circuit size.  Fix $r$ nonempty counter arcs and reconnect them
in any single cyclic order.  This gives $(r-1)!$ distinct permutations,
each differing from the counter at at most $r$ states and having one
orbit of length $N$.  For $r=2^{\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}$ with fixed
$0<\alpha<1$, the logarithm of this family size is $\Theta(r\log r)$.
The circuit-description bound used in \cref{sec:lower} implies that some
members need $\Omega(r)$ gates, while all have $O(n(r+1))$-gate circuits.
Consequently there exist families with
\[
 \CC(F_n)=2^{\alpha n+O(\log n)},\qquad
 \sup_{T\in\mathbb Z}\CC(F_n^T)=2^{\alpha n+O(\log n)}.
\]
The lower bound is existential counting, not an explicit hard transition.
Using only their transition sizes in \cref{thm:main} would instead give
an upper bound with exponential scale $2^{(1+\alpha)n/2}$.

\begin{corollary}[Charged orbit coordinates]\label[corollary]{cor:coordinates}
Suppose a bijection $\phi:\bits^n\to\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z$ satisfies
$\phi(G(x))=\phi(x)+1\pmod N$.  If a permutation $F$ differs from $G$
at $r$ states, then
\[
 \CC(F^T)\leq \CC(\phi)+\CC(\phi^{-1})+O(n(r+1))
 \quad(T\in\mathbb Z).
\]
For an $m$-bit input time, the last term is $O(n(r+m+1))$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Conjugate $F$ by $\phi$.  The exceptional-set cardinality is unchanged,
and the reference permutation becomes the counter.  Apply
\cref{thm:sparse-counter} and compose with the two coordinate circuits.
\end{proof}

Both coordinate costs are essential.  A long orbit does not itself
supply cheaply computable coordinates.  Conversely, these examples can
visit the entire state space: the compressed object is the arrangement
of arcs, without a reduction in the number of reachable states.

For the pair queries of \cref{cor:sparse-labels}, coordinate transport
needs only the forward map: encode both $x$ and $y$, then query the
conjugated permutation.  Reachability and visit times are preserved by
conjugacy, so the bound is $2\CC(\phi)+O(n(r+1))$.  No state is returned,
and no inverse-coordinate circuit is required.

A finite-field application, including the cost of discrete-logarithm
coordinates, is given in \cref{app:binary-field}.

\section{Applications}\label{sec:applications}

This section collects four consequences of the fixed-power construction:
computations with a bounded number of nondeterministic or alternating
choices, the running time of the preprocessing, a single explicit map that
collects the hardness of all linear-size maps, and the structure of
permutations whose iterator is easy but whose inverse is not.  None of these
statements is mechanized in Lean.

\subsection{A bounded number of choices}\label{sec:bounded-choice}

Let a deterministic computation have its configurations encoded in $b$
bits, as in \cref{cor:halting}, and suppose that some configurations are
\emph{choice configurations} with two successors $\sigma_0(c)$ and
$\sigma_1(c)$, while every other configuration $c$ has the single successor
$\sigma(c)$; halting configurations are absorbing.  Suppose that a circuit
of size $s$ decides whether $c$ is a choice configuration and computes
$\sigma_0(c)$, $\sigma_1(c)$ and $\sigma(c)$, and that along every path from
an initial configuration $\iota(x)$, $x\in\bits^n$, at most $g$ choice
configurations are visited.  Let $\mathrm{Conf}\subseteq\bits^b$ be a set
containing every configuration on such a path.  In an alternating
computation each choice configuration is labelled existential or
universal by the same circuit.  Acceptance is the least fixed point:
a halting configuration accepts according to its state, an existential
(universal) choice configuration accepts when some (every) successor
accepts, a nonchoice configuration accepts when its successor does, and
a configuration accepts only if this is forced after finitely many
steps.  A nondeterministic computation is the special case in which
every choice is existential.

\begin{proposition}[Bounded alternation]\label[proposition]{prop:bounded-choice}
In this setting put $b'=b+g+\ceil{\log_2(g+2)}$, $M=(g+1)|\mathrm{Conf}|$
and $A'=s+O(g\log(g+2))+b'^3$.  Acceptance on $\bits^n$ has a circuit of
size
\[
  2^{g+1}\cdot O\!\left(A'+\sqrt{b'^3MA'}\right)+O(2^g).
\]
For an in-place machine with $h$ heads, tape alphabet $\Gamma$, state set
$Q$ and $|\mathrm{Conf}|\leq|\Gamma|^n n^h|Q|$, and with $h$, $Q$ and
$\Gamma$ fixed, this is $2^g|\Gamma|^{n/2}\poly(n,g)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For a choice word $y\in\bits^g$ let $F_y$ act on pairs $(c,i)$, where
$0\leq i\leq g$ counts the choices made so far:
\[
  F_y(c,i)=
  \begin{cases}
    (\sigma_{y_{i+1}}(c),\,i+1), & c\text{ a choice configuration and }i<g,\\
    (\sigma(c),\,i), & c\text{ a nonchoice configuration},\\
    (c,i), & \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
\]
With $y$ supplied as $g$ further state bits that never change, these maps
form one map on $b'$ bits whose circuit has $s+O(g\log(g+2))$ gates: the
bit $y_{i+1}$ is selected by a multiplexer and the counter is incremented
conditionally.  The deterministic trajectory of $(\iota(x),0)$ under
$F_y$ is the computation path that resolves its $j$th choice by $y_j$.
Fix $y$.  The set of pairs reachable from $\{(\iota(x),0):x\in\bits^n\}$ is
forward invariant under $F_y$ and has at most $M$ elements.  A trajectory
in a set of $M$ states that has not reached an absorbing state after $M$
steps never does, so $F_y^M(\iota(x),0)$ is a halting configuration if and
only if the path determined by $y$ halts, and it is then the final
configuration.  By \cref{thm:invariant}, with the bits of $y$ hardwired and
$L$ optimized as in \cref{eq:L-choice} with $N$ replaced by $M$, the bit
\[
  v_y(x)=[\,F_y^M(\iota(x),0)\text{ is an accepting halting configuration}\,]
\]
has a circuit of size $O(A'+\sqrt{b'^3MA'})$.

For a prefix $u\in\bits^j$ with $j<g$, let $F^{(u)}$ be $F_{u0^{g-j}}$
modified to fix every pair $(c,j)$ in which $c$ is a choice configuration.
The trajectory of $(\iota(x),0)$ under $F^{(u)}$ follows the path with
choices $u$ until it reaches its $(j+1)$st choice configuration, and stops
there.  The same argument gives a circuit of the same size for the bit
$e_u(x)$ that says whether $F^{(u)\,M}(\iota(x),0)$ is a universal choice
configuration with counter $j$.

Now evaluate the choice tree.  Put $\mathrm{val}(y)=v_y(x)$ for
$y\in\bits^g$, and for $|u|<g$ let $\mathrm{val}(u)$ be the AND of
$\mathrm{val}(u0)$ and $\mathrm{val}(u1)$ if $e_u(x)=1$ and their OR
otherwise.  This uses $O(2^g)$ further gates.  We claim that
$\mathrm{val}(\varepsilon)$ is the acceptance bit.  By induction on $g-|u|$,
$\mathrm{val}(u)$ is the value of the configuration at which the path with
choices $u$ makes its $(|u|+1)$st choice, if it makes one.  If it makes
none, every $y$ extending $u$ determines the same path, so all leaves below
$u$ equal its acceptance bit and so does $\mathrm{val}(u)$, whatever $e_u$
is.  A path that never halts determines the value $0$, as the least fixed
point requires, because only finitely many choices occur on it.  The
circuits $v_y$ and $e_u$ number $2^g+2^g-1$, which gives the bound.  For
the in-place case, $b=n\ceil{\log_2|\Gamma|}+h\ceil{\log_2n}+O(1)$ and
a step circuit decodes each head position and updates the cells under
the heads with $O(hn)$ gates, so $s=O(hn)$, $b'$ and $A'$ are $\poly(n,g)$ and
$\sqrt M\leq\sqrt{g+1}\,|\Gamma|^{n/2}n^{h/2}|Q|^{1/2}$.
\end{proof}

The factor $2^g$ is the price of enumerating the choice words; the
dictionaries of different words $y$ cannot be shared, because their
invariant sets are disjoint.  For a binary alphabet the bound
$2^{n/2+g}\poly(n)$ is below the truth-table bound whenever
$g\leq(1/2-\varepsilon)n$ for a fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and $n$ is large.  Unbounded nondeterminism lies outside the method.
When $g$ is not bounded, the configuration graph has out-degree two, a
computation is a tree rather than a trajectory, and neither
\cref{lem:checkpoints} nor the correction identity \cref{eq:identity} has
an analogue.  Whether nondeterministic in-place machines admit circuits of
size $|\Gamma|^{n/2}\poly(n)$ is open.

\subsection{The cost of writing down the circuit}\label{sec:preprocessing}

The circuits of \cref{thm:main} are nonuniform: the checkpoint payloads
depend on all of $F$ and on $T$.  When $F$ is given by a circuit, they can
nevertheless be computed in time close to linear in $N$.  We use a
checkpoint set that is easier to compute than the path packing of
\cref{lem:checkpoints}.

\begin{lemma}[Depth-residue checkpoints]\label[lemma]{lem:residue-checkpoints}
Let $F$ be a map on $N$ states and $1\leq L\leq N$.  There is a set $H$ of
at most $2N/L$ states such that, for every $x$, the list
$x,F(x),\ldots,F^{2L}(x)$ contains an element of $H$ or a repeated state.
Given the table of $F$, such a set is computed in $O(N)$ operations.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Among the $L$ residue classes of the depths of noncycle vertices modulo
$L$, choose one of minimum size and put its vertices into $H$; this adds at
most $N_{\mathrm{tree}}/L$ vertices.  On every cycle of length
$\ell\geq L$, number the vertices in the forward direction and put those
with indices $0,L,2L,\ldots$ below $\ell$ into $H$, at most
$\ceil{\ell/L}\leq2\ell/L$ per cycle.  Leave cycles shorter than $L$
unmarked.  Then $|H|\leq2N/L$.  A trajectory starting at depth at least
$L$ meets every depth residue among its first $L$ states, hence meets $H$.
Otherwise it reaches its cycle in fewer than $L$ steps, then either meets
a mark within $L$ further steps or, on a short cycle, repeats a state
within $L$ further steps.  The cycles, the depths and the class sizes are
found by one traversal of the functional graph and one breadth-first
search from the cycles along reversed edges.
\end{proof}

Replacing \cref{lem:checkpoints} by this lemma in \cref{sec:fixed} changes
only the constant in the size of the dictionary, so it gives the bound of
\cref{thm:main} with the same $K=2L$.

\begin{proposition}[Preprocessing time]\label[proposition]{prop:preprocessing}
On a random-access machine with words of $\Theta(n)$ bits, given a circuit of
size $s$ for $F$ and the binary expansion of $T$, a circuit for $F^T$ of the
size in \cref{eq:optimized} is computed with
\[
  O\!\left(Ns+N+G+\sqrt N\ceil{\log_2(T+2)/n}\right)
\]
word operations, where $G=O(A+\sqrt{n^3NA})$ is the size of the output.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $T<K$ the circuit is $T$ copies of $F$.  Otherwise:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Evaluate $F$ on all $N$ states, $O(Ns)$ operations, and compute $H$ by
  \cref{lem:residue-checkpoints}, $O(N)$.
\item The payloads are $z_h=F^j(h)$ with the common exponent $j=T-K$.  Run a
  depth-first search on the reversed forest from each cycle vertex,
  keeping the current root-to-vertex path on a stack.  At a vertex $h$ of
  depth $d_h>j$ the payload is the stack entry $j$ positions below the
  top.  At a vertex of depth $d_h\leq j$ attached to the cycle vertex $c$
  of a cycle of length $\ell$, stored as an array, the payload is the cycle
  vertex at position
  $\mathrm{pos}(c)+\bigl((T\bmod\ell)-K-d_h\bigr)\bmod\ell$.  Cycle
  vertices are handled by the same formula with $d_h=0$.  This takes
  $O(N)$ operations once $T\bmod\ell$ is known for every cycle length.
\item The distinct cycle lengths sum to at most $N$, so there are at most
  $\sqrt{2N}$ of them.  The residue table \cref{eq:residue-table} needs
  $T\bmod p$ for $p\leq K\leq2\ceil{\sqrt N}$.  Each reduction of $T$
  modulo an $O(n)$-bit integer takes $O(\ceil{\log_2(T+2)/n})$ word
  operations by schoolbook division, so all residues take
  $O(\sqrt N\ceil{\log_2(T+2)/n})$ operations.
\item Emit the trajectory circuits, the Batcher network of
  \cref{lem:lookup} with the hardwired dictionary, the repetition and
  arithmetic circuits and the residue table, in time linear in their
  size.  Batcher's network is generated recursively.\qedhere
\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

In bit operations the bound is multiplied by $\poly(n)$.  This refines the
polynomial bound in $N$, $s$ and $\log T$ recorded in the review notes
accompanying the repository.  In particular, if a uniform family $F_n$ has
circuits of size $2^{O(n)}$ computable in time $2^{O(n)}$, and $T_n$ is
computable in time $2^{O(n)}$, then the circuits for $F_n^{T_n}$ of size
$2^{n/2}\poly(n,s)$ are computable in time $2^{O(n)}$, that is, they form an
$\mathsf{E}$-uniform family.  The same holds for the final-configuration
circuits of \cref{cor:halting}, where $T=2^b$.  Only the circuit for the
first halting time, which uses \cref{thm:depth}, has not been analysed
here.

\subsection{A universal linear-size map}\label{sec:universal-map}

Hardness of a fixed power of some linear-size map transfers to a single
explicit map with a logarithmic loss in the state width.

Fix $n$ and a constant $c\geq1$, and let $G=(c+1)n$.  Normalize every
circuit of size at most $cn$ for a map on $n$ bits to exactly $G$ gates in
topological order whose last $n$ gates are the outputs, by inserting dummy gates and then appending $n$ gates that copy the
outputs.  Such a circuit is described by
a list $D$ of $G$ entries, each an operation and two operand indices in
$[0,n+G)$, so $|D|=O(n\log n)$.  Let $U=U_{n,c}$ act on states
$(D,x,w,\mathit{pc})$ with $x\in\bits^n$, $w\in\bits^G$ and a counter
$0\leq\mathit{pc}\leq G$, of total width $m=O(n\log n)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item if $\mathit{pc}<G$, set $w_{\mathit{pc}}$ to the operation of entry
  $\mathit{pc}$ of $D$ applied to its operands, where index $i$ refers to
  $x_i$ for $i<n$ and to $w_{i-n}$ otherwise, and increment $\mathit{pc}$;
\item if $\mathit{pc}=G$, copy the last $n$ bits of $w$ into $x$, clear
  $w$ and reset $\mathit{pc}$ to $0$.
\end{itemize}
Other values of $\mathit{pc}$ are treated as $G$, and $D$ never changes.

\begin{proposition}[Universal map]\label[proposition]{prop:universal}
$U_{n,c}$ is computable in time $\poly(n)$ from $(n,c)$ and has
$\CC(U_{n,c})=O(n\log n)=O(m)$.  Put $\tau=G+1$.  For every
$F:\bits^n\to\bits^n$ with $\CC(F)\leq cn$ and every $T\geq0$,
\[
  \CC(F^T)\leq\CC(U_{n,c}^{\tau T}).
\]
Consequently, if some maps $F_n$ with $\CC(F_n)\leq cn$ have
$\CC(F_n^{T_n})\geq2^{\varepsilon n}$, then
$\CC(U_{n,c}^{\tau T_n})\geq2^{\Omega(m/\log m)}$, where the exponent
$\tau T_n$ has $O(\log T_n+\log n)$ bits.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
One step selects entry $\mathit{pc}$ of $D$ with a multiplexer over $G$
entries of $O(\log n)$ bits, $O(n\log n)$ gates; selects the two operands
from the $n+G$ bits of $x$ and $w$, $O(n)$ gates; and writes the result
through a decoder of $\mathit{pc}$, $O(n)$ gates.  The reset step and the
selection between the two cases cost $O(n)$ gates.  For the description
$D_F$ of $F$, gate $\mathit{pc}$ reads only $x$ and gates with smaller
indices, which have been written since the last reset, so
$U^{\tau}(D_F,x,0,0)=(D_F,F(x),0,0)$ and by induction
$U^{\tau T}(D_F,x,0,0)=(D_F,F^T(x),0,0)$.  Hardwiring $D_F$, $w=0$ and
$\mathit{pc}=0$ in a circuit for $U^{\tau T}$ and projecting onto $x$
gives a circuit for $F^T$ of no larger size, since constants and
projections are free.  The final claim follows from
$n=\Omega(m/\log m)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[One explicit candidate]\label[remark]{rem:universal-product}
Let $W=U_{n,c}\times P_{2k}$ act on $m+2k$ bits by
$(u,z)\mapsto(U_{n,c}(u),P_{2k}(z))$.  Every power satisfies
$\CC(W^T)\geq\max\{\CC(U_{n,c}^T),\CC(P_{2k}^T)\}$, since each factor is a
projection of $W^T$ with the other input fixed.  So this single explicit
linear-size map has a power of complexity $\Omega(2^k/k)$ by
\cref{thm:lower}, unconditionally but at an exponent of exponential length,
and at each exponent $\tau T$ it is at least as hard as every map on $n$
bits with at most $cn$ gates is at $T$, whether or not that map is
explicit.  So if any such map has a hard power at an explicit exponent
$T$ of polynomial length, the explicit map $W$ has one at the explicit
exponent $\tau T$, with only a logarithmic loss in the state width.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Asymmetric iterators}\label{sec:asymmetric}

A permutation family can make all polynomially long iterations easy while
its inverse stays hard only under strong conditions.  For a permutation
$P$ let $\ell(x)$ be the length of the cycle of $x$, $c(P)$ the number of
distinct cycle lengths, and $\lambda(P)=\ord(P)$ the least common multiple
of the cycle lengths.

\begin{definition}
A family of permutations $P_n$ of $\bits^n$ with $\CC(P_n)=n^{O(1)}$ has an
\emph{asymmetric iterator} if $\CC(\Iter_{P_n,p(n)})=n^{O(1)}$ for every
polynomial $p$, while $\CC(P_n^{-1})$ is not bounded by any polynomial.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Necessary conditions]\label[proposition]{prop:asym-necessary}
If $P_n$ has an asymmetric iterator, then none of the following is bounded
by a polynomial in $n$: the circuit complexity of $x\mapsto\ell(x)$; the
number $c(P_n)$ of distinct cycle lengths; and $\log_2\lambda(P_n)$.
Every power $P_n^{T_n}$ whose complexity is not polynomially bounded,
for instance $P_n^{-1}=P_n^{\lambda-1}$, has an exponent whose bit length
is not polynomially bounded.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $\ell(x)-1<2^n$,
$P^{-1}(x)=\Iter_{P,n}(x,\ell(x)-1)$, so
$\CC(P^{-1})\leq\CC(\ell)+\CC(\Iter_{P,n})+O(n)$.  Let
$\ell_1<\cdots<\ell_c$ be the distinct cycle lengths.  A point $x$ satisfies
$P^{\ell_i}(x)=x$ exactly when $\ell(x)\mid\ell_i$, and $\ell(x)$ is itself
in the list, so
$\ell(x)=\min\{\ell_i:\Iter_{P,n+1}(x,\ell_i)=x\}$.  With the list
hardwired this gives
$\CC(\ell)\leq c\,(\CC(\Iter_{P,n+1})+O(n))+O(cn)$.  If
$\log_2\lambda\leq p(n)$, then
$P^{-1}(x)=\Iter_{P,p(n)}(x,\lambda-1)$ with $\lambda-1$ hardwired.
Finally, $P^T=\Iter_{P,m}(\cdot,T)$ with $T$ hardwired whenever $T$ has $m$
bits.  Each implication contradicts the assumption on $P^{-1}$ when the
quantity in question is polynomially bounded.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Quantum inversion]\label[proposition]{prop:asym-quantum}
If $\Iter_{P,2n+1}$ has a circuit of size $q$, then $P^{-1}$ has a quantum
circuit of size $\poly(n,q)$ that is correct with probability at least
$2/3$ on every input.  If $P_n$ and $\Iter_{P_n,2n+1}$ are computable in
polynomial time, then $P_n^{-1}\in\mathsf{FBQP}$.  Hence a uniform family
with an asymmetric iterator would give $\mathsf{FBQP}\not\subseteq
\mathsf{FP/poly}$, and also $\mathsf{PSPACE}\not\subseteq\mathsf{P/poly}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The function $t\mapsto\Iter_{P,2n+1}(x,t)$ on $[0,2^{2n+1})$ is periodic with
least period $\ell(x)\leq2^n$ and injective on each period, so Shor's period
finding~\cite{Sho97}, or Kitaev's phase estimation~\cite{Kit95}, returns
$\ell(x)$ with probability $\Omega(1/\log n)$ using $\poly(n)$ queries.  Any
candidate $r$ with $1\leq r\leq2^n$ and $\Iter(x,r)=x$ is a multiple of
$\ell(x)$, so $y=\Iter(x,r-1)$ satisfies $P(y)=x$; the circuit checks
$P(y)=x$ and repeats $O(\log n)$ times.  For the last claim, $P_n^{-1}$ is
computable in polynomial space by iterating $P_n$, so its bit language lies
in $\mathsf{PSPACE}$ but, by assumption, not in $\mathsf{P/poly}$.
\end{proof}

So asymmetric iterators cannot be shown to exist unconditionally with
current techniques.  The natural candidates fail for a simple reason.

\begin{remark}[Fixed group elements]\label[remark]{rem:fixed-element}
Suppose $P(x)=g\cdot x$ for a group action given by a polynomial-size circuit
on encodings of group elements and points, with a fixed element $g$.  Then
$P^T(x)=g^T\cdot x$, and hardwiring an encoding of $g^T$ gives a
polynomial-size circuit for every power, including $P^{-1}$.  This covers
$x\mapsto ax\bmod M$, the RSA permutation $x\mapsto x^e\bmod N$ on
$(\mathbb Z/N)^\times$ with fixed $N$, where $g^T$ is the exponent
$e^T\bmod\lambda(N)$, Rabin squaring on the quadratic residues of a fixed
Blum integer, and the action of a fixed ideal class: all are symmetric
nonuniformly.  Making the modulus part of the state, as in the time-lock
puzzle of Rivest, Shamir and Wagner~\cite{RSW96}, removes the hardwired
group order, but then the iterator $x\mapsto x^{2^t}\bmod N$ and the inverse
are both believed hard without the factorization of $N$ and both easy with
it.
\end{remark}

The generator must therefore be part of the input, in a group in which
multiplication is easy and inversion is not.

\begin{proposition}[Groups with infeasible inversion]\label[proposition]{prop:gii}
Let $\mathcal G_n$ be groups with injective encodings into $\bits^n$, such
that the set of encodings is recognized, and multiplication is computed,
by circuits of size $n^{O(1)}$, while the map $g\mapsto g^{-1}$ has no
polynomial-size circuits.  Then $P_n(g,x)=(g,gx)$ on pairs of encodings,
and the identity on all other strings, is a permutation family with an
asymmetric iterator, and $\ell(g,x)=\ord(g)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Left multiplication by $g$ is a bijection of $\mathcal G_n$, so $P_n$ is a
permutation of $\bits^{2n}$.  For an $m$-bit $t$, square-and-multiply
computes $g^t$ with $O(m)$ multiplications, so
$\Iter_{P_n,m}((g,x),t)=(g,g^tx)$ has size $\poly(n,m)$.  With the encoding
of the identity $e$ hardwired, $P_n^{-1}(g,e)=(g,g^{-1})$, so a
polynomial-size circuit for $P_n^{-1}$ would invert group elements.
\end{proof}

Groups with infeasible inversion were introduced in the theses of
Hohenberger and of Molnar and used for directed transitive signatures and
broadcast encryption; Yamakawa, Yamada, Hanaoka and Kunihiro construct one
from indistinguishability obfuscation and factoring~\cite{YYHK14}, and
Altu\u{g} and Chen propose a candidate from isogenies over RSA
moduli~\cite{AC19}, which also surveys this history.  Whether either
candidate admits the injective encodings and recognizable encoding set
that \cref{prop:gii} needs is not settled here.  Any such group is broken
by a quantum computer, by order finding on $g$ followed by $g^{\ord(g)-1}$,
in accordance with \cref{prop:asym-quantum}.

\begin{remark}[No separation from a random power oracle]\label[remark]{rem:random-power-oracle}
Oracle separations need care with advice.  A uniformly random permutation
of $N$ points has $\ln N+O(1)$ cycles in expectation, hence $O(n)$
distinct cycle lengths with high probability.  Given the list of these
lengths as $O(n^2)$ bits of advice and the power oracle
$(t,y)\mapsto P^t(y)$, the spectrum test in the proof of
\cref{prop:asym-necessary} finds $\ell(x)$ with $O(n)$ queries, and one more
query returns $P^{\ell(x)-1}(x)=P^{-1}(x)$.  So a random permutation with
its power oracle separates iterating from inverting only for algorithms
without advice.  By \cref{prop:asym-necessary}, an oracle separation
against circuits needs a permutation with superpolynomially many distinct
cycle lengths.
\end{remark}

\section{Lean formalization}\label{sec:lean}

The accompanying \texttt{lean/} project proves existence of actual Boolean
circuits with evaluation semantics and gate bounds.  It covers
\cref{tab:results}, the invariant-set, image-sensitive and active-mass
refinements, permutation inversion, complete-configuration simulation,
and the lower-bound results.  The semantic lemmas also support arbitrary
state types with finite invariant subsets and supplied Boolean encodings;
initialization and decoding costs remain additional.

The development uses CSLib's circuits and simultaneous synthesis relation,
Mathlib's dynamics and arithmetic, and the author's Circuit library through
\texttt{complexitylib}, including its sorting and counting results
\cite{CSLib,Mathlib,Complexitylib}.  Its native basis consists of Boolean
constants, NOT, AND, and OR.  Every gate is charged, including constants;
wiring and fan-out are free.  This differs from the paper's basis by an
absolute factor and an additive constant.
\Cref{app:lean-details} states the native tradeoff and explains proof
differences.  The public theorem map, detailed constants, module guide,
pinned revisions, and build commands are maintained in \texttt{lean/README.md}.

The exported theorems have guarded axiom lists containing only
\texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice}, and \texttt{Quot.sound}, with no
\texttt{sorry} placeholders or additional axioms.  Classical choices supply
checkpoints, enumerations, and circuit witnesses; the result is nonuniform
existence.  The sparse chart's existence and circuit cost are checked, but
the edit-list compiler's algorithm and runtime, sparse-transition counting
application, and finite-field corollary remain prose results.  The
cryptographic and literature discussions are also outside the mechanized
boundary.  Kernel checking establishes neither historical priority nor
independent fidelity to every intended model convention.

\section{Conclusion and open questions}\label{sec:scope}

Sparse checkpoints and charged lookup give square-root state-space bounds
for arbitrary fixed iterates and functional-graph navigation.  The counting
example makes the exponent sharp for unrestricted fixed powers; it does not
establish sharpness for polynomial-width input times.

Two directions remain: the additive bound
$s+\widetilde O(2^{n/2})$, and broader classes of transitions with cheaply
constructible orbit coordinates.  The sparse-counter compiler shows how
an explicit structural description can supply such coordinates; finding them
from a succinct transition circuit is a separate problem.

\section{Correspondence with the formalization}\label{app:lean-details}

The native circuit model charges constants as well as Boolean operations.
Its foundational statement is the following.

\begin{theorem}[Mechanized tradeoff]\label{thm:lean}
Let $c$ be a circuit over the Boolean constant, NOT, AND, and OR basis,
with $n$ inputs and $n$ outputs, and let $S\subseteq\bits^n$ be finite
and invariant under its evaluation $F$.  For all natural numbers $T$ and
$1\leq L\leq2^n$, Lean proves the existence of a circuit $d$ agreeing
with $F^T$ on $S$ such that
\[
 |d|\leq40000\left[L|c|+(n+1)^3
       \left(L+\floor{|S|/L}+1\right)\right].
\]
Here circuit size counts every gate, including constant gates.
\end{theorem}

The harmless $+1$s cover zero-width and empty-set cases.  Define
\[
 \widehat A=s+(n+1)^3,\qquad
 \widehat B=\widehat A+\sqrt{(n+1)^3 2^n\widehat A}.
\]
The checked real optimization gives the fixed-power bound
$120000\widehat B$, and a separate theorem derives the
$2^{\lceil n/2\rceil}\poly(n)$ consequence for polynomial-size updates.
The constants are deliberately loose accounting bounds.  Free wiring
and arbitrary fan-out permit sharing; constants are charged.
Translating the paper's fixed fan-in-two basis to this basis changes
size by at most an absolute factor and an additive constant.

The public theorem map in \texttt{lean/README.md} gives the remaining exact
constants and exported names.  Three implementation differences explain how
the formalized constructions realize the paper's asymptotic bounds.

\begin{enumerate}
\item For a single fixed power, one useful collision suffices.  The circuit
sorts checkpoint and prefix records with tagged padding, then selects an
adjacent equal-key pair witnessing either a checkpoint hit or a repeated
state.  Batches use the full shared dictionary lookup of \cref{lem:lookup}.
The repeated-state decoder adds the period to a hardwired residue until the
index lies in the repeated interval; its $O(Kn^2)$ cost, with $K=2L$, fits
the main budget.
\item Cycle labels are computed after projecting by $F^{N!}$, which reaches
the cycle and preserves its labels.  A short cyclic prefix either meets a
checkpoint or contains the whole cycle, allowing a scan for its minimum and
period.  This replaces the direct first-repetition argument in
\cref{thm:labels}.  The forest proof obtains ancestry intervals by ordering
root-to-vertex paths, without implementing a traversal algorithm.
\item Sparse-counter charts use at most $r+1$ ordinary linear intervals,
with hardwired canonical cycle labels, rather than compiling the weighted
arc permutation.  Encoding and decoding handle phase wraparound within
those intervals.  Both coordinate circuits and one streaming remainder
computation are charged.  This proves the claimed circuit existence without
verifying the edit-list compiler.
\end{enumerate}

Internal arithmetic uses spare bits to represent full-length cycles and
prevent overflow.  The checked first-hitting-time circuit returns $n+1$
time bits; its bound below $2^n$ on positive instances permits discarding
the top bit.  Visit descriptors use a success flag and two $(n+1)$-bit
fields, with period zero denoting a singleton.  Their all-time semantics
and bounded-count interpretation do not include an additional
deadline-processing circuit.

\section{Sparse edits of a primitive binary-field cycle}\label{app:binary-field}

\begin{corollary}\label[corollary]{cor:binary-field}
Represent $K=\mathbb F_{2^n}$ in a fixed polynomial basis and fix a
primitive element $\omega$.  If a permutation $F$ differs from
$L(x)=\omega x$ at $r$ states, then
\[
 \CC(F^T)\leq n^{O(\log n)}+O(n(r+1))
 \quad(T\in\mathbb Z).
\]
With an $m$-bit input time the bound is
$n^{O(\log n)}+O(n(r+m+1))$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Insert zero into the nonzero multiplicative cycle by setting
$G(0)=1$, $G(\omega^{-1})=0$, and $G(x)=\omega x$ elsewhere.
Then $G$ is one cycle, and $F$ differs from it at at most $r+2$ states.
Coordinates are
$\phi(0)=0$ and $\phi(\omega^j)=j+1$ for $0\leq j<2^n-1$.
Decoding uses finite-field exponentiation and costs $\poly(n)$ gates.
Encoding uses discrete logarithms.

Kleinjung and Wesolowski's Theorem~1.1~\cite{KW22} gives expected time
$n^{O(\log n)}$ for discrete logarithms in characteristic two.
To obtain exact nonuniform circuits, let $\tau(n)$ bound this expectation
for every input.  Truncate each trial at $2\tau(n)$ steps and verify
candidate logarithms by exponentiation.  A trial succeeds with probability
at least $1/2$.  Among $n+1$ independent random tapes, the probability that
some one of the fewer than $2^n$ nonzero inputs has no successful trial is
less than one.  Fix tapes that work for every input and select the first
verified answer.  Circuit simulation and verification have polynomial
overhead in $\tau(n)$ and $n$, preserving quasipolynomial size.
Apply \cref{cor:coordinates}.  The case of bounded $n$ is absorbed by
the constants.
\end{proof}

This corollary relies on the cited discrete-logarithm theorem and the
nonuniform simulation argument; those ingredients are not formalized in
our Lean development.  It concerns sparse permutation-preserving edits
to a primitive multiplicative cycle.  It does not imply the unrestricted
conjectural bound $s+\widetilde O(2^{n/2})$.
